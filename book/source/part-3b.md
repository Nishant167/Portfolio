## 16. Q-Learning at Midnight

The assignment, on paper, is not complicated. Build an agent that learns to play a simple game — a grid world, a cart trying to balance a pole, something from the small, well-worn menagerie of environments every reinforcement learning course uses because they're simple enough to run on a laptop and hard enough to actually teach something. Give the agent a reward signal. Let it try things. Watch it get better.

The theory, from the lecture two weeks ago, is elegant in the way that theory usually is — an equation with the professor's name for it, the Bellman equation, a recursive definition of the value of being in a certain state that resolves, if you squint, into something almost philosophical: the value of where you are is the reward you get now plus the discounted value of wherever you end up next. It's a tidy idea. Deferred gratification, formalized, with a Greek letter standing in for how much the agent should care about the future versus the present.

He builds the network. A small one — a couple of hidden layers, nothing exotic, PyTorch doing the heavy lifting the way PyTorch always does, hiding a genuinely enormous amount of linear algebra behind a `.backward()` call so casual it's almost insulting to the math underneath it. He wires up the training loop: play a move, observe the result, store the experience, occasionally sample a batch of old experiences and learn from them instead of only the most recent one, because learning only from what just happened turns out to make an agent forget things it learned an hour ago, a phenomenon with its own name — catastrophic forgetting — that sounds more dramatic than it is and is, in fact, exactly that dramatic.

He runs it. And for the first several hundred episodes, nothing happens. Not nothing in the sense of an error — the code runs cleanly, no stack trace, no red text anywhere in the terminal. Nothing in the sense that the reward, plotted on a small graph he's printing to a file every fifty episodes, refuses to go up. It jitters. It dips. Around episode three hundred it looks, briefly, like it's improving, and by episode four hundred it's back where it started, as if the agent had learned something promising and then, for reasons no log file explains, decided against it.

This is the specific, particular frustration of reinforcement learning that nobody warns you about until you've lived through it: the code can be completely correct and the agent can still fail to learn, because the failure isn't a bug in the traditional sense. It's a bad hyperparameter. It's a learning rate a decimal place too high, so the network overcorrects every time and never settles. It's a reward function that technically describes what you want but leaves an exploit sitting in plain sight, which the agent — with the amoral, literal-minded thoroughness of an optimization process that has no idea it's supposed to be "trying" to win the game rather than just maximize a number — finds and abuses, racking up reward through some degenerate loop that satisfies the letter of the objective while making a mockery of its spirit.

He spends forty minutes convinced the network architecture is wrong before discovering, almost by accident, that the discount factor — the Greek letter, gamma, the one controlling how much the agent should care about the future — had been typed as 0.9 in one function and 0.99 in another, two numbers that look nearly identical on a screen and produce agents with almost opposite temperaments: one nearly myopic, one so patient it barely reacts to anything happening right in front of it. A single decimal place. Forty minutes.

There's a kind of laughter reserved specifically for this — not amusement, exactly, more a release valve, a short exhale through the nose at one in the morning when you realize the last three-quarters of an hour were spent chasing a problem that a `diff` between two functions would have caught in four seconds, if only he'd thought to run one before rebuilding the reward shaping from scratch twice.

He fixes it. The reward curve, on the next run, does the thing reward curves are supposed to do — climbs, jaggedly, with the honest noise of a system that's still exploring but is unmistakably, finally, getting somewhere. It's not a triumphant graph. It looks like a heart monitor with a mild fever, spiking and dipping around a slowly rising average. But the average is rising, and that's the entire difference between an agent that's learning and one that's just moving randomly while consuming electricity, and at 1:20 in the morning that difference feels disproportionately, almost embarrassingly large.

The CNN classifier from the same coursework, built a few weeks earlier, gave him a version of this same lesson in a gentler form — feed it enough labeled images and it learns to tell one class from another with an accuracy that climbs steadily, satisfyingly, obediently, right up until it doesn't, plateauing at a number that no amount of additional training will move, because the ceiling isn't in the optimizer anymore, it's in the data, or the architecture, or some choice made three steps upstream that a deeper network can't compensate for no matter how long you let it run. Supervised learning tells you, fairly directly, when you've hit a wall. Reinforcement learning just keeps quietly failing in a way that looks, for long stretches, exactly like success eventually arriving if you just wait a little longer.

He doesn't finish the assignment tonight. He gets the agent learning, which is not the same as getting it good — it wins more than it loses now, in the small cramped world of the grid it's confined to, and that's enough to know the pieces are correctly wired, even if the policy it's converged on is still, by any reasonable standard, a little dumb. He'll tune it more this weekend, or he won't, and either way the submission deadline is real and forgiving of imperfection in a way his own standards, at one in the morning, briefly aren't.

## 17. The Portfolio, Again

There's a version of this portfolio built entirely in Three.js, a JavaScript library for rendering three-dimensional graphics in a browser, and if you go back far enough in the commit history — which almost nobody ever does, but he has, more than once, out of a particular nostalgia for his own bad decisions — you can watch it get built, section by section, an entire 3D scene constructed to hold what is, underneath all the geometry, a fairly ordinary collection of facts about one person's career.

He does not remember exactly what he was thinking when he decided a resume needed a camera that orbits in three dimensions. He remembers the feeling more clearly than the reasoning: a conviction, strong enough to survive several weekends, that a normal scrolling page was beneath what he was capable of building, that anyone could make a page with sections on it, and that the only way to signal — to a recruiter, to himself, to nobody in particular — that he belonged in a room with people doing interesting technical work was to do something a normal page could not do.

It worked, in the narrow sense that it rendered, and it loaded, eventually, on a good connection, and it did in fact rotate. It did not work in every sense that actually mattered. It took a genuinely long time to load on anything less than a very good connection. It was, if he's honest reviewing it now, more interesting to build than it was to look at — a common and slightly humbling category of software, the kind where the process was the whole reward and the artifact itself is a footnote.

The commit that follows it in the log, months later, is blunt in a way only a commit message written for an audience of exactly one person tends to be: *Redesign away from generic AI-template look.* No elaboration. No ticket number, because there was no ticket, no manager, no design review — just him, one evening, looking at what the Three.js rebuild had eventually calcified into and recognizing, with the specific discomfort of catching yourself imitating something without meaning to, that it had drifted into looking like every other AI-adjacent portfolio built that particular season: a dark background, a gradient somewhere, a hero section promising things about "intelligent systems" in a typeface that a hundred other sites were using for the exact same promise.

He hadn't copied anyone specifically. That's what made it worse, in a way — nobody had told him to make it look like that. He'd absorbed it by osmosis, the way you absorb an accent from the people around you without noticing, from months of exactly the kind of browsing this account keeps circling back to: tab after tab of other developers' portfolios, other people's Dribbble shots, other people's "here's how I built my site" threads, until the aggregate of everything he'd looked at quietly became the thing he built, indistinguishable in retrospect from a hundred other sites built by a hundred other people doing exactly the same absorbing.

This is one of the stranger side effects of spending this many hours inside the same rabbit hole this account keeps describing: you don't just learn things. You get colonized, a little, by the aesthetic consensus of whatever corner of the internet you've been reading. And the only defense against it, it turns out, isn't reading less. It's noticing when the thing you built stops looking like a decision and starts looking like a reflex.

So: redesign. Again. A data-engineering visual theme this time, an attempt to make the site actually *about* the thing it was claiming to represent — pipelines instead of orbiting spheres, a visual language borrowed from the diagrams he draws at work instead of from whatever the current aesthetic consensus of "AI portfolio" happened to be that month. Better. Not perfect. Nothing built at eleven p.m. between a full workday and an M.Tech module is ever going to be perfect, and by now he's mostly made peace with that, in the particular way you make peace with something you keep doing anyway.

## 18. Borrowing a Language

The version of the site that exists now — the one an actual visitor would land on — didn't start as a data-engineering idea at all. It started as someone else's real-estate dashboard.

Nestio, a property-listing platform he came across during some completely unrelated evening of browsing, had a design language he couldn't stop thinking about: a persistent sidebar, a warm cream canvas instead of the reflexive dark mode every developer portfolio defaults to, a terracotta accent color doing more work than any single hue has any right to, generous rounded corners, soft diffused shadows that made cards feel almost tactile. It was built to sell apartments. He wanted to describe a career in data pipelines. On paper, these have nothing in common.

What he actually did, once the idea took hold, was closer to translation than design — the specific, oddly satisfying puzzle of finding the equivalent word in a different vocabulary. A property card, in the original system, showed a photo, a price, a bed count, a square footage. His version needed to show a project, a technology stack, a scale, a business impact. Not the same information. The same *shape* of information — a headline fact, two or three supporting facts, a visual anchor — and once he saw the shape underneath the particular content, the translation almost built itself. Price became "years of experience." Beds and baths became pipelines built and data volume processed. The listing's "available now" badge became an uptime percentage, ninety-nine point nine, a number that is, incidentally, completely true of the systems he actually maintains, which felt like a small, private joke — a decorative UI flourish that happened to also be an accurate fact about his actual job.

There is something genuinely pleasurable about this kind of constraint, more pleasurable than starting from a blank canvas ever is. A blank canvas asks you to invent a whole design language from nothing, an infinite space of decisions with no wrong answers to rule anything out, which sounds freeing and is actually paralyzing — he knows this from the Three.js version, from staring at an empty scene for twenty minutes trying to decide what color the void should be. A borrowed system, by contrast, gives you rules. Twenty-pixel radii. This specific shadow, this particular spacing scale. The terracotta accent appears here and only here. Working inside somebody else's carefully considered constraints turns out to produce better decisions, faster, than working inside the total freedom of nothing at all — a lesson that has nothing to do with real estate and everything to do with why so much of his actual engineering work, the reconciliation tooling, the pipeline architecture, gets easier the moment somebody hands him a schema to work within instead of an open field.

He rebuilds the whole thing in Next.js, a framework upgrade that turns out to be its own small ordeal — new routing conventions, a rendering model that behaves differently depending on which file you put a component in, an App Router that took him most of an evening just to stop actively fighting. Tailwind for the styling, tokens defined once in a theme file so that "terracotta" means the exact same hex value everywhere it's used instead of drifting slightly every time he eyeballs a new component. Chart.js for the two data visualizations — a donut chart breaking down his skill mix, a dual-line chart showing, in a shape that's more honest than most resume bullet points, the rough trajectory of his time between data engineering and AI over the last several years.

None of it is difficult, exactly. Most of it is just long — the particular kind of long that comes from caring about details nobody asked you to care about, adjusting a shadow's opacity by two percentage points and staring at it for longer than two percentage points has ever deserved, because at eleven-forty on a Thursday there is nobody to tell you the shadow was fine at the first value and the decision to keep adjusting it belongs entirely to you.

## 19. The Scroll That Wouldn't Trigger

GSAP — the GreenSock Animation Platform, a JavaScript library for exactly the kind of scroll-triggered reveal animations that make a modern portfolio site feel alive instead of static — is, by most accounts, one of the more forgiving animation libraries to work with. He believes this is generally true. He does not believe it was true for him, specifically, on the Thursday night his ScrollTrigger instances stopped firing entirely, for no error, no warning, nothing in the console except the calm, undisturbed silence of code that thinks it's working.

The problem, once he finds it — after a genuinely embarrassing amount of time spent assuming the bug lived somewhere in his own animation configuration — turns out to be structural rather than syntactic. His page doesn't scroll the way a browser normally scrolls. He'd built the whole layout around Lenis, a smooth-scrolling library, wired specifically to an inner content column rather than the window itself, because the design called for a persistent sidebar that stays fixed while only the main content area moves. Which is a perfectly reasonable design decision, and also exactly the kind of decision that quietly breaks a library like GSAP's ScrollTrigger, whose entire model of the world assumes, by default, that scrolling happens on the window, the way scrolling has happened on every ordinary webpage since the dawn of ordinary webpages.

He spends over an hour convinced the fix has to be in his ScrollTrigger configuration — some option he's missing, some flag he hasn't set, tabbing through documentation, trying variations of the same broken assumption over and over with the particular, specific stubbornness of someone who has decided the problem must be small because the fix, if it exists, is probably small too. It's a bad heuristic. Problem size and fix size are not reliably correlated, and he knows this, professionally, from years of watching one-character configuration errors take down entire production pipelines, and he still falls for the inverse of it constantly: assuming a bug that's taking a long time to find must eventually reveal itself as complicated, when very often it doesn't. It just reveals itself as somewhere else than you were looking.

The actual fix, once he finds it in a GitHub issue buried four pages into a search — someone else's four-star repository of suffering, essentially, a stranger who'd hit the exact same wall and left a note about the door — is a single configuration option: telling ScrollTrigger explicitly which element to treat as the scroller, instead of letting it assume the window. One line. Scoping every single trigger on the page to the actual scrolling container instead of a phantom one that was never moving in the first place.

It works immediately, completely, almost insultingly well, the way fixes do when you've spent an hour looking in the wrong place and five seconds looking in the right one. Sections fade in as they enter the viewport. Numbers count up from zero. A progress ring — the same one from months earlier, the one currently sitting frozen at seventy-two percent in a data file he wrote and half-forgot — draws itself onto the screen for the first time, stroke by stroke, exactly the way he'd imagined it working back when it was still just a number in a TypeScript object with nothing animating it yet.

He watches it happen four or five times in a row, refreshing the page each time purely to watch the ring draw itself again, which is not an efficient use of a Thursday night and is, nonetheless, exactly what he does. There's no ticket that required this. No client asked for it, no manager will ever know a ring exists, let alone that it took an hour of misdirected debugging to make it move correctly. It is, by every external measure available, a completely pointless use of an evening.

He refreshes it a sixth time anyway.

## 20. Four Projects, One Night

By half past one, the machine has four things running that have nothing to do with each other, and somehow, tonight, none of them have been abandoned yet — a state that does not last, and that he will remember afterward as one of the better versions of this particular chaos, before it curdles, as it usually does, into the kind of scattered evening that produces four half-finished things instead of one finished one.

One terminal tab is running the reinforcement learning training loop from earlier, the reward curve still climbing in its jagged, honest way, occasionally glanced at rather than watched, the way you check on something in the oven without opening the door. Another is the local dev server for the portfolio, hot-reloading every time he nudges a spacing value half a pixel in one direction and then, ten minutes later, nudges it back. A third is a Python REPL where he's poking at the RAG pipeline's retrieval quality, testing whether the sentence-boundary chunking fix from the night before holds up against a new batch of questions he didn't have time to try it on then. A fourth — the newest, the one that actually started the whole evening off — is the TKI agent, sitting mid-conversation with itself in a test harness, waiting on a tool call to a service he hasn't finished wiring up yet, which means the agent is currently, technically, stuck, and has been stuck for the last twenty minutes, patiently, the way only software can be patient, without any of the low creeping anxiety a person would feel being stuck for that long.

None of this looks impressive from outside. If someone photographed his desk right now — nobody is going to, there's nobody here to hold the camera — it would show one person, one chair, two monitors, and absolutely no visual evidence of four separate technical threads being held loosely in one skull at the same time. That's the part of building at this hour that resists demonstration. It doesn't compress into a screenshot. The interesting part isn't on the screen. It's the specific mental posture of holding four unfinished things without any of them fully collapsing, switching between a Bellman equation and a Tailwind spacing token and a chunking strategy and a stalled tool call, in the space of ninety seconds, without any one of them demanding the others be abandoned first.

He knows, somewhere underneath the momentum, that this isn't the efficient way to do any of it. Focus, in the productivity-book sense, would say: pick one. Finish it. Then the next. He has read that advice, nodded at it, generally agreed with it in daylight, and has never once, on a night like this, actually followed it, because the truth — the one that doesn't fit neatly into a listicle — is that the four things are feeding each other. The RL training loop taught him something about reward shaping twenty minutes ago that just quietly reframed how he's thinking about the agent's tool-selection logic. The portfolio's chart component, half-built, gave him an idea for how to visualize the RAG pipeline's retrieval scores if he ever gets around to building that. Nothing here is wasted, exactly, even the parts that look, from the outside, indistinguishable from unfocused sprawl.

It's not sustainable. He knows that too. Somewhere past two, this same posture is going to stop feeling like momentum and start feeling like exactly what it also, simultaneously, already is — four unfinished things, no closer to done than they were three hours ago, at an hour when a version of himself with better judgment would have picked one and gone to sleep having actually finished something.

But that's not tonight's problem. Tonight, at half past one, four terminals are open, a reward curve is climbing, a ring is drawing itself on refresh, and nothing has broken badly enough yet to demand a decision.
