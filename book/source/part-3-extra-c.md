## The Tool the Agent Refuses to Use Correctly

Months into the TKI agent's development, well past the evening it first learned to tell the time, he adds a tool that should, by every reasonable expectation, be simpler than most of what already works: a calculator, exposing basic arithmetic to a model that is, famously, unreliable at arithmetic on its own, precisely the kind of gap a tool is supposed to close cleanly.

It doesn't close cleanly. What actually happens, across a frustrating stretch of testing, is that the model uses the calculator tool inconsistently — reaching for it correctly on some queries, and on others, for reasons he can't immediately pin down, simply computing the arithmetic itself, wrong, with exactly the same fluent confidence it would have used if the tool had never been added at all. The tool exists. The model has been told, explicitly, in the system prompt, that arithmetic should always go through it. And on a meaningful fraction of runs, it just doesn't bother, guessing instead, and guessing badly.

He spends an evening convinced this is a prompting problem — that some phrasing of the instruction would finally close the gap, the same way a slightly different config flag closed the GSAP scroll bug months earlier. He tries a dozen variations, more emphatic each time, at one point resorting to something close to all-caps insistence in the system prompt, a technique he's slightly embarrassed to admit trying and slightly more embarrassed that it produces a measurable, if imperfect, improvement, as if the model responds, in some genuinely strange way, to something resembling being shouted at.

The real understanding, once it arrives, comes from a different direction entirely — not a prompting trick, but a shift in how he'd been thinking about the problem. The model isn't failing to notice the tool exists. It's making a probabilistic judgment, learned from an enormous corpus of examples where humans answer arithmetic questions directly, all the time, without reaching for a calculator, because the arithmetic in question is simple enough that reaching for a tool would look, to a human reader, like overkill. Adding two small numbers together is exactly the kind of task a fluent human would just answer, and the model, trained extensively on fluent human text, has absorbed that same instinct — use the tool for the hard cases, skip it for the easy-looking ones — without any awareness that its own internal arithmetic, unlike a human's, is actually unreliable exactly on the cases that look easiest.

This reframes the whole problem, and the fix that follows from the reframing is smaller and stranger than anything he'd tried before: rather than insisting harder that the tool always be used, he adjusts the framing so the model has less reason to trust its own instinct that a given calculation is "easy enough" to skip — removing any language that implies simplicity, presenting every arithmetic request as part of a context where precision matters enough that guessing, however confident, isn't an acceptable substitute for checking. It isn't a perfect fix. Nothing in this whole project has been a perfect fix. But the calculator gets used reliably enough, after that, that he stops noticing the gap in ordinary testing, which is about as close to victory as most of this work ever actually gets.

There's something almost funny, once he's through it, about the specific shape of the failure — a computer, whose entire premise is doing arithmetic perfectly and instantly, occasionally declining to use its own perfect arithmetic tool because it had absorbed, from human writing, a very human instinct that some sums are too easy to bother checking. He doesn't stretch the observation into anything grander. He just notices it, files it next to the other small, specific, slightly uncanny things this project has taught him about what a language model actually is underneath the fluency, and moves on to whatever's next on the list he never finishes.

## Debugging in a Language He Doesn't Speak Well

The four-star board-game repository, mentioned back in an earlier chapter, is written in a language he uses rarely enough that reading it properly, months later, when he finally decides to actually adapt a piece of its PDF-parsing logic for his own use, takes considerably longer than reading the equivalent Python would have — and teaches him something in the process that fluent skimming, in a language he already knows well, would never have surfaced.

He can read the syntax; it's close enough to languages he does know that the shapes are recognizable, functions and loops and conditionals in slightly unfamiliar clothing. What he can't do, at first, is skim — the fluent, pattern-matching kind of reading he does effortlessly in Python, where his eyes jump straight to the parts that matter and gloss over the boilerplate because years of exposure have taught him exactly which parts of a Python file are almost always boring. In this unfamiliar language, every line demands the same deliberate attention, because he hasn't yet built the intuition for which parts are safe to skip.

This turns out, unexpectedly, to be an advantage rather than a cost, for this particular purpose. Reading slowly, forced to actually parse every line instead of pattern-matching past most of them, he notices something in the original author's PDF-parsing logic that a faster read would very likely have missed: a specific, deliberate choice to process pages in reverse order, from the end of the document backward, with no comment explaining why. It looks, on first glance, like an arbitrary quirk, maybe even a small bug. Reading further, slowly, he finds the reason buried three functions later — board-game rulebooks, it turns out, disproportionately put their most commonly-referenced content, the quick-reference tables and edge-case rulings, toward the back, in an appendix, and the original author had apparently noticed this pattern across enough rulebooks to optimize for it deliberately, prioritizing the pages most likely to contain the answer to a typical query.

It's a genuinely clever piece of domain-specific tuning, invisible to anyone skimming fast enough to miss the unexplained ordering choice, and he only caught it because his unfamiliarity with the language forced a slower, more literal read than his fluency in Python would ever have permitted. He adapts the idea, not the code, into his own RAG pipeline's chunk retrieval — a small adjustment to weight certain document sections based on where domain-specific content tends to cluster, a trick he'd never have found in the specification or the documentation, because it was never written down anywhere except as an unexplained ordering decision inside a stranger's code.

He's started, since, occasionally reading unfamiliar codebases in languages he barely knows on purpose, specifically for this reason — not to learn the language, though some of that happens incidentally, but because the forced slowness produces a different, more attentive kind of reading than fluency ever allows. Fluency is efficient. It is also, he's realized, a filter, quietly deciding in advance what's worth his attention before he's had a chance to actually look, and the filter, however well-calibrated for ordinary reading, misses exactly the kind of quiet, undocumented cleverness that a slower, more humble read sometimes catches by accident.
