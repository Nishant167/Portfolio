## Mentoring Someone Who Reminds Him of Himself

A junior QA analyst joins an adjacent team roughly a year into his current role, young enough in her career that she's still writing the kind of exhaustive, slightly over-eager test cases he remembers writing himself at Kalaage Creations, and their paths cross often enough, over shared tooling and the occasional cross-team question, that something resembling an informal mentorship develops without either of them ever naming it that explicitly.

She asks him, early on, a version of a question he's been asked before and never quite answered well: how do you know when you've tested enough. It's a real question, not a rhetorical one, and it doesn't have a clean answer, because the honest answer — you don't, not really, you just develop an increasingly calibrated sense of where the likely failures hide and stop chasing the unlikely ones past a certain point of diminishing return — sounds, said plainly, like a non-answer, a shrug dressed as wisdom, exactly the kind of thing a more experienced colleague says when they don't actually remember what it felt like not to know.

He tries harder than that, for her sake, and ends up articulating something he's never had to put into words before, precisely because nobody had ever pushed him to: testing enough isn't a state you reach, it's a rate you're chasing — the rate at which each additional hour of testing is still finding things worth finding, versus the rate at which it's returned to finding nothing, or finding things too unlikely or too low-stakes to matter. The skill isn't knowing when you've covered everything. Nobody ever covers everything; the space of things that could go wrong is, for any system complex enough to be worth building, larger than any test suite could ever fully enumerate. The skill is noticing, in real time, when your rate of finding real problems has dropped enough that your next hour would be better spent elsewhere.

She asks a good follow-up, the kind of question that reveals someone's actually thinking rather than just collecting an answer to repeat later: how do you build that sense of "where the likely failures hide" in the first place, before you've been burned by enough of them to have it. He doesn't have a shortcut for her, and he tells her so directly, because pretending there's a shortcut would be a disservice — the sense gets built the slow way, by being burned, by finding the discrepancy that everyone else missed and by missing the one everyone else found, by accumulating enough specific, remembered failures that a new system starts to feel, faintly, like it rhymes with one you've seen fail before, in a way that isn't fully articulable and doesn't arrive any faster for wanting it to.

What he doesn't tell her, because it feels too indulgent to say out loud to someone he's supposed to be helping rather than confessing to, is that watching her ask these questions is doing something for his own understanding that he didn't expect going in — forcing him to articulate, plainly, principles he's operated on instinctively for years without ever once having to defend them to anyone, the same specific discipline the "explain it to someone who doesn't code" exercise from an earlier chapter produced, now happening unprompted, in real conversations, with someone whose actual questions are sharper and less predictable than anything he'd have thought to ask himself in a private rehearsal. Mentoring her, it turns out, is teaching him more about what he actually knows than most of the courses he's started and never finished.

## The Conversation He Rehearsed and Never Had

There's a raise he considered asking for, roughly eighteen months into the current role, armed with the twenty-percent efficiency figure and a genuine, defensible case for why his contribution had outgrown his compensation — and he spent, by his own rough accounting, more actual hours rehearsing the conversation in his head than the conversation, had he had it, would ever have taken to actually complete.

He drafted the case in a document nobody else ever saw, laying out the reconciliation tooling's measured impact, the migration validation work, the specific incidents caught before they became client-facing problems, structured the way he'd structure a technical proposal, with evidence organized in the order most likely to build a persuasive case rather than the order it had actually happened in. He rehearsed opening lines, several different ones, in the shower, on a walk, in the particular idle moments he has spent a lot of time documenting other uses for. He imagined objections and prepared responses to them, an entire imagined dialogue running several rounds deep, conducted entirely with a version of his manager who existed only in his own head and who he'd built, without quite meaning to, into someone considerably more skeptical and harder to convince than the actual person had ever given him reason to expect.

He never had the conversation. Not because he lost his nerve outright — though there was some of that too, if he's honest — but because a reorganization happened first, his reporting line changed, a new manager arrived who didn't have the context the rehearsed conversation had been built for, and by the time enough time had passed to rebuild that context with someone new, the moment, whatever specific shape it had once had, had passed, and the case, still true, still defensible, no longer felt like it belonged to a specific conversation he was building toward. He raised his compensation eventually, through the ordinary channel of an annual review cycle, without ever deploying a single one of the rehearsed lines, the actual conversation, when it finally happened, taking about six minutes and following none of the structure he'd spent so many idle hours perfecting.

He thinks about this sometimes as its own small, specific example of a pattern that shows up everywhere in this account under different names — the evaluation dashboard built instead of the fix, the eleven courses started instead of finished, the four projects held open simultaneously at half past one. Rehearsing the raise conversation was, in its own way, a kind of building — genuine effort, genuine thought, a real case constructed with real care. It just never got deployed against the actual problem it was built for, overtaken by circumstances outside his control before it ever got the chance, and the actual resolution arrived through an entirely different, much less carefully engineered channel. He's stopped assuming, since, that the hours he spends preparing for something are hours that will necessarily get used the way he planned. Sometimes they don't. The preparation still happened. It just ends up filed, quietly, next to everything else he has described that got built and never quite finished the way it was originally supposed to.
