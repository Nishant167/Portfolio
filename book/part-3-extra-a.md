## Shipping It Somewhere That Isn't His Laptop

The minimal ChatGPT clone works perfectly on his machine, which is, he's learned the hard way across enough projects to stop being surprised by it, a claim that means almost nothing about whether it'll work anywhere else. "Works on my machine" is close to a joke at this point in the industry, repeated with the specific weary irony of people who've been burned by the gap between local and deployed often enough to find it funny rather than infuriating. Tonight, deploying the clone somewhere a friend could actually try it without cloning a repository and setting up a Python environment themselves, he gets to rediscover exactly why the joke exists.

Docker is supposed to close this gap — package the application, its dependencies, and enough of its environment into a single portable image that "works on my machine" becomes, in theory, "works on any machine that can run this container," a promise that's genuinely kept often enough to be worth the overhead of learning the tool properly. He writes a Dockerfile. It's not his first — he's containerized things before, at work, where someone else usually owns the base image and he's mostly just adding application code on top of infrastructure decisions that were made before he arrived. This is the first time he's built one from a truly empty file, deciding every layer himself, and the decisions turn out to matter more than he expected.

The first version of the image is enormous — several gigabytes, for an application whose actual source code is a few hundred lines of Python. He'd started from a full, general-purpose base image, the kind that includes an entire operating system's worth of tools he doesn't need, on the theory that starting from something complete would minimize the chance of a missing dependency causing a mysterious failure later. It works. It's also comically bloated, slow to build, slower to push, and takes long enough to pull down on a test connection that he starts to understand, viscerally rather than academically, why every serious deployment guide he's ever skimmed insists on starting from a slim base image instead.

He rebuilds it from a minimal image, adds only what the application actually needs, and watches the size drop by an order of magnitude — a satisfying number to watch shrink, the kind of optimization that has an immediate, visible, undeniable payoff, unlike a lot of the more abstract cleanup work this book has spent time describing. There's a genuine craft to this that he hadn't expected to enjoy as much as he does: figuring out exactly what a piece of software needs to run, stripped of every convenience and assumption a bloated starting point had quietly been carrying for it, until what's left is close to the actual minimum, legible in a way the original never was.

Getting the container running locally is the easy half. Getting it running somewhere that isn't his laptop — a small cloud instance, cheap enough that the monthly cost is a rounding error against his salary and expensive enough that leaving it running by accident would eventually annoy him — introduces an entirely new category of problem, mostly involving networking, environment variables that exist locally in a `.env` file and don't magically transfer themselves to a remote host, and a firewall rule he forgets to open on the first three attempts, each attempt producing the exact same unhelpful symptom: the container running, by every metric the platform can show him, and the application, from the outside, simply not answering.

He finds the missing rule the way he finds most things at this hour — not by insight, by elimination, ruling out the container itself, then the application code, then the port binding, before finally checking the one layer he'd assumed was fine because he hadn't touched it: the instance's own network configuration, still set to its restrictive defaults, blocking exactly the port his application was listening on. One setting. Another one of those forty-minute detours that resolves, once found, in about four seconds.

It works, eventually, at an hour he doesn't bother checking. A friend, the next day, tries it from an entirely different country, on an entirely different network, and it answers — a small, unglamorous, completely real proof that the container did what it was supposed to do, which is travel intact from his machine to a stranger's, carrying its entire environment along with it, exactly as promised, after several hours of discovering exactly how many ways that promise can quietly fail to be kept before it finally is.

## FAISS, Pinecone, and the Cost of Choosing

The vector database decision for the RAG pipeline sounds, from a distance, like it should be simple: pick the tool, move on, the actual interesting work is supposedly the retrieval logic sitting on top of it, not the storage layer underneath. In practice, the decision eats most of an evening, because the two obvious options — FAISS, a library rather than a service, running entirely on his own machine, and Pinecone, a managed service somewhere in the cloud, handling the infrastructure so he doesn't have to — represent two genuinely different philosophies about where complexity should live, and he spends longer than he'd like to admit going back and forth between them before settling on an answer that feels, in the end, less like a discovery and more like a decision he had the power to make all along.

FAISS is free, in the sense that matters most to a personal project with no revenue attached to it — no monthly bill, no usage tier to worry about outgrowing, nothing running anywhere he doesn't control. It's also, he learns fairly quickly, considerably more work to operate correctly than the phrase "just a library" initially suggests. Persistence isn't automatic; he has to think, explicitly, about how the index gets saved and reloaded between runs, a detail that a managed service would have simply handled for him, invisibly, as part of what he'd be paying for. Scaling past what fits comfortably in memory on his own machine would require him to solve problems that Pinecone has already solved, behind an API he'd never have to think about, for a monthly fee proportional to how much thinking he wanted to skip.

Pinecone, in exchange for that fee, hands him something that works within minutes — an API key, a client library, an index created with a single function call, queries returning results before he's had time to properly read the documentation he opened to understand them. It's a genuinely pleasant experience, the kind of frictionless onboarding that makes a strong first impression and, if he's honest, makes him slightly suspicious of exactly how frictionless it is, the way any experienced engineer gets quietly wary of anything that seems too easy at the start, on the theory that the difficulty postponed at the beginning tends to resurface later, usually at a worse time and often attached to a bill.

He ends up with FAISS, not because it's objectively better — for a project with real users and real scale, Pinecone's case is genuinely strong, and he'd probably choose it if this were work rather than a personal project — but because the entire point of building the RAG pipeline himself, rather than just reading about how other people built theirs, was to understand what's actually happening underneath the abstraction. Pinecone, for all its convenience, would have handed him a black box exactly at the layer he most wanted to understand. FAISS forces him to think about indexing strategy, about the tradeoff between exact and approximate nearest-neighbor search, about what actually happens when a query vector gets compared against a hundred thousand stored ones, in a way a managed API was never going to require of him.

This is, he recognizes, a slightly perverse way to choose a tool — optimizing for what he'd learn rather than what would ship fastest — and it's not a choice he'd defend in a professional context, where shipping fast and not reinventing infrastructure someone else has already built well is usually the correct call, the one his day job would expect of him without a second thought. But this isn't the day job. This is the one part of his week where the metric isn't velocity, and choosing the harder, more instructive tool, purely because it's more instructive, is one of the very few luxuries the whole arrangement actually buys him.
