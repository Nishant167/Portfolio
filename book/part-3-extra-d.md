## The Prompt That Worked by Accident

There's a specific, slightly uncomfortable phase every builder of language-model applications passes through, and he passes through it fully on the TKI agent somewhere around its third month: the discovery that a prompt he wrote, which had been working reliably for weeks, was working for a reason entirely different from the one he'd assumed, and that the assumption, wrong as it was, had never once been tested because the outcome had never once given him a reason to test it.

The prompt in question instructs the model on how to decide whether a user's request requires a tool call at all, or whether it can simply be answered from what the model already knows. He'd written it early, tested it against a handful of examples, watched it perform correctly, and moved on — the same pattern that governs most of software development, professional or personal, where a thing that works gets trusted roughly in proportion to how long it's kept working, regardless of whether anyone ever verified why.

The unraveling starts when he adds a new category of question the agent hadn't previously encountered, and watches the tool-selection logic make a specific, confident, wrong call — reaching for a tool it didn't need, on a question that should have been answerable directly. Debugging why leads him, eventually, to reread his original prompt more carefully than he has in months, and he notices something that should have bothered him from the beginning and somehow never did: the instruction he'd written wasn't actually teaching the model a general principle for when to use a tool. It was, almost accidentally, closely matching the specific phrasing pattern of the handful of test examples he'd validated it against, months earlier, in a way that generalized far less robustly than the passing tests had led him to believe.

This is a specific, recognizable trap in this field, one he's read about in the abstract and is now encountering directly, viscerally, in his own work rather than in someone else's case study: a prompt, or a model, that appears to have learned a general rule when it's actually just pattern-matched closely enough to a narrow test set that the narrowness never got exposed, because nobody happened to test outside the narrow band until reality eventually did it for them. His original handful of validation examples weren't wrong, exactly. They just weren't diverse enough to reveal that the prompt's apparent success was riding on a coincidence of phrasing rather than a genuinely robust instruction.

The fix requires more than a tweak — a properly expanded evaluation set, deliberately adversarial this time, built specifically to probe the edges rather than confirm the center, phrased in ways designed to break the pattern-matching shortcut rather than reinforce it. The rewritten prompt that eventually passes this harder test is longer, less elegant, considerably less satisfying to read than the original — good prompts, he's noticed, often look almost too simple, and the simplicity that felt like a virtue in the original version turns out, in retrospect, to have been part of what let it get away with matching a narrow pattern instead of encoding an actual rule.

He carries this forward into how he validates everything else in the project from that point on, treating a prompt's early success with more suspicion than he used to, specifically because the failure mode here is so quiet — nothing crashes, nothing errors, the system just confidently does the wrong thing in exactly the cases nobody thought to test, which is, on reflection, the same quiet failure mode that's cost him time in every other part of this book, wearing yet another disguise good enough that he needed to be burned by it directly before he started reliably checking for it in advance.

## Overfitting, Explained by a Bad Habit

The M.Tech coursework introduces overfitting formally, with equations and a specific vocabulary — a model that's learned the training data too well, memorizing its particular noise and quirks instead of the underlying pattern, performing beautifully on data it's already seen and badly on anything new. He understands the definition on the first pass, the way he understands most formal definitions on a first pass: correctly, abstractly, without yet having felt it happen to something he built himself.

He feels it happen a few weeks later, on a small side project unrelated to the coursework — a simple classifier, built as a weekend exercise, meant to sort a personal collection of scanned documents into rough categories. The training accuracy, watched across successive epochs, climbs beautifully, approaching what looks, on the graph, like a triumphant near-perfect score. He's pleased with this for exactly as long as it takes him to check the number that actually matters, the accuracy on documents the model hasn't seen during training, and finds it stalled, mediocre, nowhere near what the training curve implied it should be — a gap between the two numbers that widens, if anything, the longer he keeps training, the model getting better and better at recognizing the specific documents it's already memorized and no better at all, possibly slightly worse, at recognizing anything genuinely new.

This is the formal definition, arriving as an actual felt experience rather than an abstraction on a slide, and the gap between those two modes of understanding turns out to be larger than he expected going in. He'd have said, honestly, before this weekend, that he understood overfitting. He understood the words. What he hadn't understood, not really, not in his hands, was the specific, seductive shape of the mistake from the inside — how genuinely good the training curve looks while it's happening, how easy it would be, watching only that curve, to declare victory and ship a model that's actually much worse than the curve implies, simply because nobody thought to check the number that would have revealed the gap.

He starts noticing, once he's felt this directly, versions of the exact same shape showing up in places that have nothing to do with machine learning at all — a personal habit he's had for years, without ever having the vocabulary to name it, of getting extremely good at answering the specific questions a familiar client asks, in the specific way that client tends to ask them, in a way that would very likely fail badly if a new client, with unfamiliar priorities and an unfamiliar way of framing a question, showed up expecting the same fluency. Competence that's actually memorization of a narrow, familiar pattern, dressed convincingly as generalized skill, until something genuinely new arrives and reveals the gap the training curve was never designed to show.

He doesn't overcorrect into paranoia about this — most competence is genuinely general, most of the time, and treating every skill as secretly overfit would be its own kind of unproductive anxiety. But he's started, deliberately, seeking out the equivalent of a held-out validation set in his own professional life: new clients, new domains, new categories of problem, chosen partly because they're interesting and partly, now, because they're the only reliable way to find out whether what looks like broad competence on the training data of his everyday work would actually hold up against something it's never seen before.
