## The Skill List, Rank Ordered

The skills section of the portfolio presents each category with a percentage — Azure Databricks and PySpark at ninety-five, Python and SQL at ninety-two, AI and LLM engineering at eighty-five, machine learning at eighty, DevOps and infrastructure at seventy-five — a set of numbers he built, like the ring and the donut chart before them, more by feel than by any formal assessment, and that he's spent an unreasonable amount of time, across several separate evenings, quietly second-guessing.

The trouble isn't picking a number for any one skill in isolation. It's that the moment he assigns Azure Databricks a ninety-five, every other number on the page has to be judged relative to it, and he finds himself running an odd, recursive kind of self-evaluation he'd never have volunteered for if a spreadsheet cell hadn't demanded it: is he really only eighty percent as capable in machine learning as he is in the platform he uses every single working day, or is that comparison meaningless on its face, two entirely different kinds of competence being forced onto the same one-dimensional scale because a UI component needed a single number to draw a progress bar with.

He settles, eventually, on a private, informal rubric that never makes it into any commit message or comment, something closer to: could he walk into a room tomorrow and be immediately, unambiguously useful on this specific thing, versus would he need a running start. Azure Databricks and PySpark: no running start required, seven-plus years of daily reps behind it, he could debug a stranger's broken pipeline within the hour. Machine learning: he'd need the running start, not because he doesn't understand the concepts — the M.Tech has made sure of that — but because the specific, hands-on fluency that only comes from doing something under real pressure, repeatedly, for years, simply hasn't accumulated yet the way it has for the data engineering skills that pay his bills.

This distinction — understanding versus fluency, knowing the theory versus having the calluses — turns out to be the actual, honest logic underneath every number on the page, once he manages to articulate it to himself clearly enough to stop second-guessing the percentages every time he looks at them. It's not a measure of how smart he is about a given topic. It's closer to a measure of how many actual hours of friction he's already absorbed on it, which is a genuinely different axis, and conflating the two — assuming a high score means "understands well" rather than "has been burned by this many times already" — is exactly the kind of category error a recruiter skimming the page for eight seconds is almost certain to make, and that he's decided, after all this deliberation, he can't actually control from his side of the screen.

He leaves the numbers as they are, not because he's confident they're correct, but because he's decided, finally, that "correct" was never actually available as an option for a chart like this one, only "honest enough to defend if pressed," which is a lower bar and, he's come to think, the only bar this specific kind of self-assessment was ever going to be able to clear.

## The Thing About Titles

There's a persistent, low-grade discomfort he's never fully resolved about the word "Engineer" attached to his current title, one he's never voiced to a colleague because voicing it would require explaining a distinction most people, reasonably, don't spend much time thinking about: the difference between engineering a system from scratch and engineering the checks that prove a system someone else built is behaving correctly.

Both are real, valuable work. He doesn't want to undersell what he does by contrast with some imagined "real" engineering happening somewhere else, a comparison he's watched other people in adjacent, more traditionally "builder" roles make dismissively about QA and reconciliation work, usually people who've never had to explain to a client why their migration is trustworthy, a task considerably harder than it sounds. But there's a genuine, structural difference between the person who decides how a pipeline should transform data and the person who verifies, independently, that the transformation actually happened the way it was supposed to, and his career, examined honestly across every title on it, has spent more of its total hours in the second role than the first, regardless of what any individual job title happened to say.

This isn't false advertising, exactly — "Data Engineer" is an accurate enough description of what he does day to day, and plenty of what he builds is genuine, from-scratch engineering: the reconciliation tooling itself, the automated CI gate, the migration validation frameworks, all things he designed and built rather than merely checked. But there's a version of the title that would be more precise and less marketable, something closer to "the person who makes sure the engineering other people did is actually correct," and he's noticed, turning this over, a small, persistent tension between the title that gets him taken seriously in a room and the title that would actually, accurately describe the specific shape of the value he provides.

He's decided, on balance, that this tension isn't worth resolving by changing anything — the portfolio says Data Engineer, the résumé says Data Engineer, and both are true enough to stand behind without hedging every time someone asks what he does. But he thinks, sometimes, about a hypothetical, more honest job title nobody would ever actually post a listing for — Chief Reconciler, maybe, half joke and half accurate — and finds a specific, private satisfaction in the fact that the joke title, unmarketable as it is, might be the only one on record that would have made his seven-year-old test-case-writing self at Kalaage Creations immediately, instantly understand exactly what the job still, underneath every subsequent title, actually is.
