## The Viva

The M.Tech's evaluation structure includes, alongside the written assignments and the DQN-style project submissions, an occasional oral examination — a viva, in the terminology the program uses, borrowed from a much older academic tradition than anything else in a curriculum otherwise dense with acronyms barely a few years old. A professor, live, on a video call, asking questions about a submitted project until satisfied the candidate actually understands what they built rather than having assembled it from tutorials without absorbing the reasoning underneath.

He finds this format more nerve-wracking than any written exam he's taken in years, for a specific reason that takes him a while to articulate properly: a written assignment can be revised, polished, checked against documentation before submission, presenting the cleanest version of his understanding rather than the actual, live, sometimes stumbling version that exists in his head at any given moment. A viva has no revision pass. The professor asks a question about why he chose a particular discount factor for a reinforcement learning project, and the answer either exists, clearly, immediately, in his own working understanding, or it doesn't, and there's no tutorial to quietly consult mid-conversation, no forty minutes of quiet googling available the way there would be on an ordinary debugging evening.

The specific question that catches him off guard, in the viva for the DQN project described several chapters back, isn't about the discount factor bug he'd already fixed and could discuss fluently. It's a follow-up, almost casual, about what would happen to the agent's learned policy if the reward function were changed slightly after training had already converged — whether the existing policy would still be meaningfully useful, or whether it would need to be retrained from scratch. He hadn't thought about this specific question before, not because it's obscure, but because his actual project had never required him to, the training having run exactly once, to exactly one reward function, with no reason to consider what would happen under a change he'd never actually made.

He answers slowly, working it out in real time, out loud, in front of the professor, which is a specific kind of exposed he hasn't experienced since his very earliest QA interviews years ago — reasoning through an unfamiliar question live, with someone experienced enough to immediately notice if the reasoning goes somewhere unjustified, no time to construct the tidier, more confident-sounding version he'd have produced given twenty minutes alone with a search engine. The answer he arrives at, eventually — that a policy trained against one reward function would likely transfer partially but not fully to a meaningfully different one, retaining useful behaviors that happen to still be rewarded while missing new priorities it was never trained to notice — turns out to be roughly correct, confirmed by the professor's brief, satisfied nod before moving to the next question.

What stays with him afterward isn't the specific answer, which he's since half-forgotten the details of. It's the format itself, the specific discipline of being made to reason live, unrevised, in front of someone qualified to catch a bluff immediately — a discipline his actual working life, full of Slack messages he can draft and delete before sending and tickets he can research quietly before responding to, rarely demands of him anymore, and that he'd half-forgotten how uncomfortable, and how clarifying, it actually is to be asked a real question with no time to construct the polished version of the answer first.

He's started, since, occasionally forcing a version of this discipline on himself deliberately — explaining a technical decision out loud, unscripted, to nobody in particular, before writing it down, purely to check whether the fluent written version he's about to produce is actually built on understanding that would survive being asked a follow-up question live, or whether it's the kind of confident-sounding fluency this entire book has spent a great deal of time learning to distrust, in himself as much as in anything he's ever asked a language model to produce.
