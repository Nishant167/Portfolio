## The Migration That Didn't Lose Anything

Cloud migration validation, on paper, sounds like the least interesting phrase in his entire job title's supporting vocabulary — a compliance-flavored task, the kind of work that shows up in a job description as a bullet point nobody reads closely. In practice, on the specific week this book borrows from, it's the closest his day job comes to the exact tension this book keeps returning to: the difference between a system that appears to work and a system that's actually been proven to.

The migration in question moves a substantial slice of historical data from an aging on-premises system into the Azure Databricks lakehouse his team maintains — the kind of project that gets budgeted in months, not days, and that nobody outside the data team will ever think about again once it's finished, provided it's finished correctly. His job, specifically, is not moving the data. Other people, other pipelines, handle the actual transfer. His job is proving that what arrived is what left — the same reconciliation instinct from every earlier chapter, scaled up to a volume where "just eyeball it" stopped being a viable strategy years before he ever touched this project.

He builds the validation the way he builds most things at work by now: methodically, unglamorously, starting with the boring checks before allowing himself anywhere near the interesting ones. Row counts first — does the new system have the same number of records the old one had, partitioned the same ways, for the same date ranges. Then aggregate checks — do the sums, the averages, the distinct counts agree across a representative sample of the fields that actually matter to somebody downstream. Only once those pass does he move to the harder, more interesting layer: spot-checking individual records, tracing a specific row through the entire migration path, watching for the kind of subtle transformation error that aggregate statistics are specifically bad at catching, because a value that's wrong in one direction and a different value that's wrong in the opposite direction can cancel out in a sum and leave the aggregate looking perfectly healthy while individual rows underneath it are quietly, completely incorrect.

He finds exactly this, three days into the validation — not a large-scale failure, nothing that would have shown up in the aggregate checks at all, but a specific subset of records, small enough as a percentage that it very nearly didn't get sampled, where a timezone conversion had been applied inconsistently between two source tables that were supposed to already be in the same timezone and, it turns out, quietly weren't, for reasons that trace back to a system configuration decision made years before he started this job, by someone whose name isn't attached to any documentation he can find.

Finding it is not dramatic. There's no eureka moment, no triumphant chapter-ending discovery — just a Thursday afternoon, a spreadsheet, and a pattern that took looking at enough individual rows, patiently, to notice at all. Fixing it requires coordinating with a team he doesn't normally work with directly, explaining the discrepancy clearly enough that they can trace it back to the source system's original configuration, and re-running a slice of the migration once the fix lands upstream. The whole process takes the better part of two weeks, most of it waiting on other people's schedules rather than active work on his part, the unglamorous administrative texture of enterprise software that never makes it into any story about engineering.

When it's done, the client — whoever they are, whatever brand this particular slice of advertising data belongs to, information he'll never have and doesn't need — gets a migration that moved a substantial volume of historical data with, as far as anyone can now verify, zero silent corruption. Nobody outside the data team will ever know this was a real risk that got caught rather than a formality that was always going to pass. That's precisely the job. The best possible outcome of a cloud migration validation is complete, total invisibility — a client who never has reason to wonder whether their historical numbers can still be trusted, because somebody, unglamorously, checked.

## The Efficiency Number, Explained Properly

The twenty-percent efficiency figure that sits on his résumé, mentioned early in this book and referenced a few times since without ever being fully unpacked, deserves, at this point, an honest explanation rather than a bullet point — partly because the honest version is more interesting than the bullet point suggests, and partly because the process of arriving at it says something true about how this kind of number actually gets made, as opposed to how it gets presented.

The reconciliation process it describes used to be manual — not entirely, but substantially: an analyst, or more often him, running a set of queries by hand against two systems, exporting the results, comparing them in a spreadsheet, flagging discrepancies for follow-up. It worked. It also took a predictable, non-trivial chunk of time every cycle, time that scaled roughly linearly with the volume of data being checked, which meant that as the client's data grew, the manual process was quietly becoming more expensive to run at exactly the rate the business least wanted an internal process getting more expensive.

He built tooling to automate the comparison — not a dramatic rewrite, mostly a set of parameterized queries and a script that ran them against both systems automatically, formatted the discrepancies into a readable report, and flagged anything above a configurable threshold for a human to actually look at, rather than requiring a human to look at everything regardless of whether anything was actually wrong. The twenty percent, when he finally measured it properly rather than estimating it for a résumé bullet, came from timing the old manual process across several cycles, timing the new automated one across the same number of cycles, and subtracting — the same boring, unglamorous measurement discipline that produces every number in this book he actually trusts.

What the résumé bullet doesn't capture, because no résumé bullet captures this kind of texture, is that the twenty percent understates the real value in one direction and overstates it in another. It understates it because the automated version doesn't just save time — it catches things a tired analyst running the fortieth manual comparison of the month might reasonably miss, the exact kind of subtle timezone-conversion error from the migration validation, the kind that hides inside aggregates and only shows up if someone's actually paying full attention on the specific cycle it happens to appear. It overstates it because twenty percent, as a single clean number, implies a precision the underlying measurement doesn't really have — a handful of timed cycles, extrapolated, rounded to a number that sounds credible without pretending to be more rigorous than it actually was.

He's made peace with presenting an imperfect number cleanly, the same way he's made peace with the portfolio's invented skill-mix percentages and its seventy-two percent ring. A résumé bullet isn't a scientific paper; it isn't supposed to carry error bars. But he knows, privately, exactly how much rigor sits behind the specific twenty in "twenty percent," and it's more than most résumé numbers get and less than the confident, unhedged phrasing implies — which is, he suspects, roughly true of almost every impressive-sounding metric anyone has ever put on a résumé, his own included.
