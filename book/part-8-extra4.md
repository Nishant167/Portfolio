## The Question His Manager Actually Asked

There's a one-on-one, unremarkable in every way except for a single question near its end, that stays with him longer than most of the more formally significant conversations from the same stretch of his career — his manager, closing out a routine check-in, asks, almost as an afterthought, what he'd build if the company gave him a month with no other obligations and complete freedom to choose the project.

He answers honestly, which surprises him slightly even as he's doing it — not a polished, résumé-ready answer about scaling the reconciliation tooling or expanding the migration validation framework, the kind of answer that would sound appropriately ambitious in a performance review. He describes, instead, something close to the actual TKI agent, translated into a version that could plausibly serve the company rather than just himself: an internal tool that lets analysts query the medallion architecture's gold-layer tables in plain language, using exactly the tool-calling pattern he's spent the better part of a year building and rebuilding on his own time, for an audience of nobody.

His manager's response is more interested than he expects, and the conversation that follows — genuinely unplanned on either side, running fifteen minutes past its scheduled end — is the first time in this entire account that the badge-on version of him and the badge-off version get to be in the same room together, even briefly, even informally, describing to a professional audience something that's only ever existed, until this exact conversation, in the specific hours this book has spent so much time documenting as private.

Nothing dramatic follows immediately. There's no project greenlit on the spot, no sudden merger of his personal and professional work into a single unified effort — the reality of a company's priorities, reasonably, doesn't reorganize itself around one engineer's evening project just because a manager found it interesting in a one-on-one. But something smaller and real does shift: a seed gets planted, mentioned again a few weeks later in a different context, that eventually, months on, becomes an actual small internal pilot, built on work hours this time, informed directly by everything he'd already learned the hard way on his own time about tool registration and prompt reliability and the specific, quiet ways a language model declines to use a tool correctly even when explicitly told to.

He thinks about this conversation, sometimes, as the one moment in this whole account where the wall between the two versions of himself — described plainly in an earlier chapter as two people sharing one badge — got a door cut into it, briefly, unexpectedly, by a single genuine question that happened to actually want the honest answer rather than the polished one. He doesn't think this happens often, and he's not sure it would have happened at all if he hadn't, by that point, already put in enough unwitnessed hours that the honest answer, when finally asked for, was actually ready and specific rather than vague and aspirational. The evenings didn't get built for this moment. But when the moment arrived, unplanned, they were there, quietly, to draw from.

## An Ordinary Set of Notifications

Some Sunday mornings, badge fully off, no lecture queued, no client wall to route around, he checks GitHub the way other people check the weather — not expecting anything in particular, just curious what's accumulated since he last looked.

A dependency needs updating, on a project he'll get to eventually or won't. Someone starred a repository — not the TKI agent this time, an older, smaller thing he'd half-forgotten existed, a script he wrote over a year ago to solve a problem specific enough that he's genuinely surprised anyone else ever needed it, and pleased, in the same small, disproportionate way this book has documented a dozen times by now, that a stranger found it useful enough to mark. There's a comment on an old, closed issue, from someone encountering the same problem months later, thanking a fix he barely remembers writing.

None of this is dramatic. The M.Tech is still ongoing, the never-finished list is still longer than what's been crossed off it, the tab graveyard has already, predictably, reconstituted itself past thirty since the last time anyone bothered counting. It's just an ordinary notification panel, on an ordinary Sunday, containing the same small, scattered, mostly unglamorous texture that fills most of his actual weeks, dressed up in none of the drama a good story would want to give it.

He closes the tab, eventually, and goes to make coffee, or doesn't, and the day continues in whatever unremarkable shape a Sunday takes when nothing in particular is scheduled. Nothing here resolves anything. It was never really trying to.
