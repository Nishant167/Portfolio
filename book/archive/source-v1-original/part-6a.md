# PART VI — THE OTHER KIND OF PRODUCTIVITY

## 35. Research Is Not Building

There is a version of an evening that looks, from the outside — from inside his own head, even, in the moment — indistinguishable from every productive evening described so far in this account. The laptop is open. Tabs are multiplying. Something technical is being read, closely, with real attention. By any surface measure, this is exactly what a night of learning is supposed to look like.

The difference, the one that only becomes visible in retrospect, is that nothing gets built. Not that night, not the next one, sometimes not at all. He reads an entire long-form comparison of vector database options — Pinecone versus FAISS versus a handful of newer entrants he'd never heard of before that evening, feature matrices, benchmark charts, threads where people argue with real conviction about indexing strategies for use cases he does not currently have and may never have. He reads it thoroughly. He takes it in. He could, if quizzed, explain the tradeoffs competently the next morning.

He does not need this information. That's the part that takes an uncomfortably long time to admit. The RAG pipeline sitting half-finished in another folder already uses FAISS, chosen weeks ago for reasons that were sound at the time and remain sound now, and nothing about tonight's reading changes that choice, informs that choice, or improves anything currently running. It is not research in service of a decision. It's research in the shape of research, performed with all the outward signs of productive learning, in service of nothing he can point to except the particular, private comfort of feeling like he's staying current.

He has a name for this now, arrived at slowly, over enough nights like this one to notice the pattern: research is not building, and the two feel almost identical from inside the chair, which is exactly what makes the substitution so easy to make without noticing. Building has resistance. Building has a moment, usually within the first twenty minutes, where something doesn't work and a decision has to be made about how to make it work, and that moment carries a specific, low-grade discomfort that research simply doesn't have. Research just flows. One article leads cleanly to the next, no error messages, no failed test suite, no code refusing to compile at the exact moment you're most tired and least equipped to figure out why. It's the frictionless version of learning, and frictionless things are, by their nature, extremely easy to keep doing long after they've stopped producing anything.

This isn't a case against reading. He'd defend, forcefully, the actual value of half the rabbit holes in this account — the MCP article that started the whole TKI project, the four-star repository that taught him more about restraint than a hundred well-funded ones would have. Those nights of reading led somewhere. What he's trying to name, carefully, without letting the naming curdle into a blanket self-criticism he doesn't actually believe, is the particular subset of reading that doesn't lead anywhere and was never really trying to. The kind that exists to produce the feeling of forward motion without the actual motion, a treadmill dressed as a road.

He closes the vector database comparison article at some point past midnight, having learned a genuinely interesting fact about approximate nearest-neighbor search that he will, with high confidence, never use, and opens the RAG pipeline's actual code instead — the thing that's been sitting half-finished for three nights, waiting for a decision about how to handle documents that don't chunk cleanly along sentence boundaries, a decision that has nothing to do with anything he just read and that reading had, if he's honest about it, been quietly avoiding making by staying safely upstream of it in the comfortable territory of general knowledge.

It's a small test he's developed, imperfect but useful: before opening any new article, ask what decision it's supposed to inform. If there's a real answer — this article will tell me whether to use approach A or approach B for the specific chunking problem sitting open in another tab — the reading is probably worth doing. If the honest answer is something vaguer, something closer to "this seems like the kind of thing I should know," that's usually the tell. Not always wrong to read anyway. Just worth noticing, in the moment, which kind of reading it actually is.

He doesn't always pass his own test. Nobody passes their own test every night; that would make it a rule instead of a test, and rules, in his experience, are exactly the kind of thing that get quietly abandoned around eleven p.m. on nights when the resistance of actual building feels like slightly too much to take on. But noticing the difference, even imperfectly, even after the fact rather than before, turns out to be worth something. It's the difference between a habit that's invisible to you and one you're at least occasionally catching yourself in the middle of.

## 36. The Tab Graveyard

At any given moment, across two windows he rarely closes entirely, there are somewhere between twenty-five and forty tabs open, and if he's honest — actually honest, not performing self-deprecation for a laugh — he could not currently tell you what more than a third of them are for.

He does an inventory once, on a slow Sunday, mostly out of curiosity about his own habits rather than any genuine intention to fix them. The results are, in their way, a small archaeological record of several weeks of exactly the kind of nights he has been describing. Three tabs are various states of the same Stack Overflow question about a PySpark partitioning quirk, opened, apparently, on three separate occasions by three separate past versions of himself who each forgot, independently, that he'd already searched for this before and half-solved it a different way each time. Two tabs are competing tutorials for the same library, never fully read, kept open on the theory that comparing them would be useful, a comparison that has now been pending for eleven days. One tab is a job listing at a company he has no intention of applying to, kept open for reasons even he can't reconstruct — morbid curiosity about the salary range, probably, closed a full paragraph after he opens it to check.

Four tabs are genuinely still active — the RAG evaluation notebook, the TKI agent's tool registry documentation, a reference for a Tailwind utility class he can never quite remember the name of, and the four-star board-game repository from months ago, still open, still occasionally glanced at, functioning at this point less as a reference and more as a kind of talisman, a reminder of a particular evening rather than a resource he's actually consulting.

The rest — and it is, doing the actual count, the majority — are what he starts privately calling the tab graveyard: pages opened with real intention, in a moment of genuine curiosity, that then simply never got closed, not because they're still needed but because closing a tab requires a small decision — *I am done with this, it can go* — and making that decision, it turns out, takes a specific kind of mental energy that's in shorter supply at midnight than the energy required to just leave it there and open another one instead.

There's something almost taxonomic about a browser window left this way for long enough. You can read, in the accumulated tabs, the actual shape of several weeks of scattered attention far more honestly than any retrospective account of "what I worked on this month" would produce. The tabs don't lie the way memory does. Memory smooths a month into a tidy narrative — I was working on the RAG pipeline, then the agent, then the portfolio. The tabs show the truth underneath that narrative: four things pursued in parallel, at overlapping and often contradictory levels of seriousness, abandoned and returned to and abandoned again, in an order no retrospective summary would ever choose to admit to.

He closes eleven of them in the Sunday cull, mostly the duplicates and the ones he genuinely cannot explain the presence of. He does not close all forty. Some part of him — not a part he's proud of, exactly, but not one he's willing to fully override either — treats an open tab as a kind of unfinished promise, and closing it without having honored the promise feels like a small defeat even when the promise was never realistic to begin with. The board-game repository survives the cull. So does the job listing, for reasons he still can't articulate on the record.

By the following Thursday, the count is back up past thirty. This is not a story with a tidy resolution where he installs a tab manager and solves the problem permanently. He has tried tab managers. He currently isn't using one. The graveyard reconstitutes itself with the reliability of weather, and at some point — not tonight, not with any real conviction behind the decision — he mostly stops trying to prevent it and starts, instead, just periodically walking through it, the way you'd walk through an actual graveyard: not to clean it up, just to notice what's there.

## 37. Renaming the Section

The commit history on the portfolio has a small, almost comic sequence buried in it, spread across a handful of days: a Services section gets added with eight offerings — data engineering, web development, app development, AI solutions, QA and testing, ETL and data testing, design and UI/UX, portfolio development — and then, days later, gets renamed to Expertise, and two of the eight offerings quietly disappear from the list entirely.

Nobody asked for this section. No recruiter emailed requesting a services list; the site isn't a freelance storefront, he isn't taking client work through it, there is no button anywhere that lets a visitor actually hire him for "Design & UI/UX," a category he added and then, less than a week later, removed, presumably on the theory that claiming design as an area of expertise felt like overreach the moment he looked at it again with slightly more skepticism than the night he wrote it.

He can reconstruct the reasoning, roughly, from memory rather than from any note he left behind, because none of this got written down anywhere except in the commits themselves, terse and unexplained. The eight-item list felt, at the time it was built, like breadth — proof that he could do more than one thing, a hedge against the narrow read of "just a data engineer" that the rest of the site, built carefully around exactly that identity, was otherwise pretty firmly committed to. Then, a few days of looking at it, the breadth started reading less like range and more like diffusion — a list trying to be everything to everyone, which is a particular and recognizable failure mode of anyone building something with no editor but themselves.

"Portfolio Development" is the one that embarrasses him slightly in hindsight, rereading the commit log cold. He'd added it, apparently, as a service offering, on a portfolio site, which is either a small, self-referential joke he doesn't remember intending or an entirely unironic addition made at an hour when the recursive strangeness of it — a website about him offering, as a service, the building of websites about other people — simply didn't register. He removes it in the same commit that renames Services to Expertise, which reads, in retrospect, like the correction of an error he never consciously identified as an error, just felt as a vague wrongness he fixed before he'd fully articulated why.

This whole sequence — add eight things, second-guess two of them, rename the header, ship it, presumably look at it again later and adjust something else — is not really a story about a website. It's a story about the specific difficulty of being your own editor on something that represents you, where every decision about what to include is also, quietly, a decision about who you're claiming to be, and there's no colleague in the room to say "that's a stretch" before it ships, only a version of yourself a few days later doing the job a colleague would have done, at a cost of a live commit that anyone could theoretically go back and read.

He has left the sequence in the git history rather than squashing it into something cleaner. Partly laziness — squashing history takes more effort than leaving it. Partly, if he's honest, because there's something he likes about the fact that it's there: eight, then six, an eyebrow-worthy title briefly claimed and then quietly withdrawn, all of it visible to anyone who goes looking, a small, undramatized record of a person changing their mind about how big a claim they were comfortable making about themselves.

## 38. The Excuse With Good Documentation

There's a particular trap he's fallen into more than once, one that's harder to see than ordinary procrastination because it wears procrastination's exact opposite clothing: the excuse with good documentation. Not avoiding the work. Doing a different, adjacent piece of work instead, thoroughly, with real craftsmanship, in a way that produces something genuinely useful and completely sidesteps the actual thing that needed doing.

The clearest example lives in the RAG pipeline's evaluation tooling, which is, by any honest measure, more polished than the pipeline it's evaluating. He built a small dashboard — nothing elaborate, a script that generates a readable HTML report after each evaluation run, faithfulness and relevance scores charted over time, color-coded, genuinely pleasant to look at. He spent two full evenings on it. It has better documentation than almost anything else in the repository, a README section explaining exactly how to interpret each metric, written with the specific care he usually reserves for things he actually expects other people to read.

Nobody was going to read it. There is no other person on this project. And the thing the dashboard was supposedly in service of — improving the pipeline's actual retrieval quality, the chunking strategy that's been sitting at "good enough" for weeks without anyone, meaning him, actually revisiting it — didn't move an inch during either of those two evenings. He'd built an excellent instrument for measuring a problem while leaving the problem itself completely untouched, and the instrument was good enough, polished enough, documented thoroughly enough, that it felt like real progress the entire time he was building it.

This is the part that makes it dangerous in a way ordinary procrastination isn't. Ordinary procrastination announces itself. You know, watching a video instead of working, that you're not working — there's no ambiguity, no plausible cover story. The excuse with good documentation doesn't announce itself, because it isn't lying to you about what it is; it's genuinely useful, genuinely well-built, genuinely the kind of thing that would appear on a list of things accomplished this week if he were keeping such a list. It's just not the thing. And the fact that it's not the thing is very easy to lose track of when the thing-that-isn't-the-thing is this satisfying to build.

He notices it, eventually, the way he notices most of his own patterns: too late to prevent it, in time to name it. Looking at the evaluation dashboard's commit history next to the chunking logic's — one actively developed across two long, careful sessions, the other frozen at the exact same "good enough" state it's been at for three weeks — the gap is not subtle once he's looking directly at it. He'd been so busy building a better way to measure the problem that measuring had quietly become a substitute for solving it.

He doesn't fix the chunking logic that night either, for the record, because naming a pattern honestly and immediately correcting it are two different accomplishments and he's only actually managed the first one. But naming it turns out to matter more than it initially seems like it should. The next time he catches himself two evenings deep into a beautifully documented tool that's adjacent to a problem instead of inside it, he recognizes the shape faster. Not fast enough to stop before it starts. Fast enough, usually, to stop before it becomes a third evening.

## 39. Ambition as Noise

Some nights the problem isn't procrastination at all, dressed up or otherwise. It's the opposite failure, one that looks, from a distance, indistinguishable from drive: too many real, legitimate ideas arriving at once, each one genuinely worth pursuing, none of them getting pursued far enough to actually finish, because the arrival of the next idea keeps outpacing the completion of the last one.

He has, at any given time, a mental list — never fully written down, because writing it down would require confronting its actual length — of projects he has genuinely intended to build. An extension to the TKI agent that would let it maintain state across sessions instead of starting fresh every conversation. A proper benchmark suite for comparing embedding models on domain-specific text instead of the generic leaderboards everyone quotes. A tool, half-sketched on a notes app during a commute, for visualizing how a PySpark job's execution plan actually branches, because the built-in visualizers at work are technically accurate and practically unreadable. A rewrite of the portfolio's chart components using a library he read about three weeks ago that promised better performance for a problem the current charts don't actually have yet, at his current traffic, which is close to zero.

Every one of these is a reasonable idea. That's precisely the problem. None of them is obviously worse than the others, which means none of them wins the competition for his attention decisively enough to actually get finished, and the result — if he tracks it honestly across a month rather than a single night — is four or five projects each roughly thirty percent built, instead of one or two actually shipped.

He starts to think of this, privately, as ambition behaving like noise rather than signal — not the absence of drive, an oversupply of it, undifferentiated, every idea arriving with the same urgent, plausible-sounding case for why it deserves to be next. A single compelling idea is a signal; you know what to do with it. Six compelling ideas arriving in the same week aren't six signals. They're static, and static, however energetic it looks from outside, doesn't move anything forward. It just hums.

There isn't a clean fix for this, and he's suspicious, again, of anyone who claims there is — the productivity-industrial complex has an entire genre of content promising exactly this fix, usually in the form of a system, a framework, a matrix with two axes that will finally tell him which idea deserves his Tuesday nights. He's tried a few of these. They work for about a week, and then the next genuinely interesting idea shows up, ignores the matrix entirely, and wins his attention anyway, because the matrix was never actually deciding anything — he was, informed by nothing more rigorous than which idea currently itches the most.

What actually helps, on the rare nights it helps at all, is smaller and less systematic: picking one thing, out loud, even if the only audience for the sentence is himself, and treating every other idea that shows up that same evening as something to write down rather than something to chase immediately. A parking lot, not a graveyard — the tabs get closed, eventually, but the ideas themselves get one line in a notes file, unglamorous, half of them never revisited, a few of them waiting there for the exact right later evening when they'll suddenly make more sense than everything currently competing for his attention.

It's not discipline, exactly. He wouldn't claim discipline; discipline implies a level of consistency he doesn't actually have. It's closer to triage, performed imperfectly, by someone who knows the ideas will keep arriving faster than he can build them and has mostly made peace with that ratio instead of expecting it to change.
