## The Marquee Nobody Reads

There's a small, purely decorative component on the portfolio — a horizontal band of technology names, scrolling continuously, right to left, forever, at a speed he tuned by hand: Azure Databricks, PySpark, Delta Lake, Python, SQL, LangChain, Hugging Face, PyTorch, TensorFlow, Docker, Kubernetes, Terraform, FAISS, Pinecone, Azure DevOps, looping back to the beginning the moment it reaches the end, an infinite procession of logos and words that exists purely so a visitor's eye has something moving to land on for a second or two before scrolling further down the page.

He spends an entire evening on the speed of this thing. Not the content — the list itself took ten minutes to write, a straightforward inventory of what he actually uses. The speed. Fifty pixels per second, according to a comment he left in the code for his own future reference, calculated from the total width of all the items divided by how long he wanted one full loop to take, a formula he derived specifically so that adjusting the list later wouldn't require re-guessing the timing from scratch.

Nobody has ever commented on the marquee's speed. Nobody ever will. It is, almost definitionally, the least consequential decision on the entire site — a visitor's eye slides across it for at most two or three seconds before moving on to something that actually contains information, and whether it moves at fifty pixels per second or sixty would register, to that visitor, as no difference at all. He knows this. He tuned it anyway, adjusting the duration constant four separate times across one evening, watching it loop, deciding it read as slightly too fast, slowing it down, deciding the slower version dragged, landing, eventually, on a number that felt — and this is the whole of the justification, felt — correct.

This is, if he's being genuinely honest about the shape of an evening like this one, not efficient use of time by any external measure. There is no metric on earth that would reward four iterations spent on a scrolling ticker's velocity. And yet there's something he trusts about the impulse underneath it, even while recognizing how disproportionate it looks from outside: the belief that craft lives in exactly this kind of decision, the ones nobody will ever consciously notice and everybody would, in aggregate, unconsciously feel the absence of if enough of them were made carelessly instead. A site made of a hundred small decisions like this one reads, even to a visitor who couldn't articulate why, as considered. A site made of a hundred careless equivalents reads as slightly off, in a way that's hard to point to and easy to feel.

He's aware this reasoning could excuse almost any amount of unnecessary polish, and he's tried, with mixed success, to hold a line between "this detail matters to the feel of the whole" and "I am procrastinating on something harder by fussing over something easy and satisfying." The marquee, he's decided, sits on the right side of that line — small, finished within a single evening, not standing in for anything more difficult he was avoiding. Not every fussed-over detail earns that verdict. This one, he still believes, looking back, actually does.

## The List He Never Finishes

There's a document — not a real to-do app, just a plain text file, unglamorous on purpose, sitting in a folder he doesn't back up anywhere clever because losing it would honestly be a mild relief — that has been accumulating project ideas for over a year, and he has never, not once in that year, gone back and crossed one off because it got finished.

That's not quite fair to the list. A few of the ideas on it did eventually get built — the TKI agent is on there, in its earliest form, a single line from a night before the project had a name: "agent that uses tools instead of hardcoded API calls — look into that MCP thing." But the ideas that got built are a small minority against the ones that just sit there, entry after entry, most of them written in the same clipped, unpunctuated shorthand of someone typing fast at an hour when full sentences feel like more ceremony than the thought deserves: "chrome extension that summarizes long threads." "port the reconciliation logic to a general-purpose library, could actually be useful to other people." "visualize spark execution plans properly, the builtin UI is unreadable." "try building something with the new realtime API, see what latency is actually like." "learn Rust properly instead of just reading about it for the fourth time this year."

He rereads this list occasionally, usually on nights like the ones described in the last several chapters, when four projects are open and none of them feel like quite the right one to be spending the evening on, and the list, more often than not, makes him feel two contradictory things at once with an intensity that surprises him every time. There's a real, uncomplicated excitement — every single line on this list is something he'd genuinely enjoy building, none of it is filler, none of it was written to pad the document out. And there's something closer to dread, quieter, harder to name cleanly, at the sheer accumulated weight of it: dozens of good ideas, each one representing real hours he doesn't currently have, each one a small, specific admission that his curiosity generates raw material faster than his evenings can process it, and that this gap isn't closing, isn't going to close, is if anything widening every month as the list gets longer than the rate at which anything gets crossed off it.

He's tried, a few times, to treat the list as a problem to be solved — a backlog to work through methodically, oldest entries first, the same disciplined triage he applies to actual work tickets. It's never once worked. The list doesn't respond to discipline the way a work backlog does, because nothing about it is actually owed to anyone; there's no client waiting on "chrome extension that summarizes long threads," no consequence to it sitting there for another eighteen months exactly as unbuilt as it is tonight. What actually moves an item off the list, on the rare occasions anything does, is never triage. It's exactly the mechanism all of this keeps describing — some unrelated Tuesday, some other rabbit hole, circling back around and making one particular old idea suddenly, urgently interesting again, for reasons that have nothing to do with how long it's been waiting its turn.

He's stopped feeling guilty about the list's length, mostly. Not entirely — there's still a flicker of something uncomfortable every time he adds a new line without removing an old one, the mathematics of it obviously, permanently unfavorable. But he's come to think of the list less as a queue and more as something closer to a compost heap: most of what goes in there stays exactly where it lands, slowly becoming less current, less exciting, quietly irrelevant as the field around it moves on. And every so often, unpredictably, something in there turns out to still be alive, and grows into something real, and the only way to have that happen even occasionally was to keep writing lines into the file that would mostly never be read again.
