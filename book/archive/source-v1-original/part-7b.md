## 46. Betting on a Protocol Nobody Had Heard Of

There's a specific kind of risk in spending real, finite personal time on something that might turn out to be a footnote — a protocol, a framework, a tool that seems significant at the exact moment you commit to it and might, eighteen months later, be a name nobody remembers, a line in a "whatever happened to" thread written by someone with more free time than mercy.

He knew this risk existed when he started building on MCP, and he built on it anyway, for reasons that are hard to fully defend on paper and easy to feel from inside. Not certainty that it would become standard — he had no way to know that, and still doesn't, fully, even now. Something closer to a bet on shape: the particular problem MCP was solving, the lack of a common language between models and tools, was real, obviously real, the kind of gap that doesn't stay unfilled for long once enough people notice it. Something was going to fill it. Maybe not this exact protocol, maybe a competitor, maybe a completely different approach nobody had proposed yet. But the shape of the eventual solution, whatever ended up winning, was likely to rhyme with what he was already looking at, and building fluency with the current attempt seemed likely to transfer even if the current attempt didn't survive.

That's a hedge, not a conviction, and he's aware of the difference. A true believer builds on a new protocol because they're certain it's the future. He built on it because the cost of being wrong seemed low and the cost of not trying seemed, in a quieter way, higher — not a financial cost, a curiosity cost, the specific regret of having watched something interesting happen early and having chosen, out of caution, not to get close enough to understand it before everyone else did too.

There's a difference, he's come to think, between conviction and stubbornness that only reveals itself under pressure, and the pressure, in this case, arrived in the completely ordinary form of a slow month — three weeks where the TKI agent's development stalled, where a promising early demo didn't lead anywhere further, where the honest, unglamorous truth was that the tool wasn't yet doing anything meaningfully better than what a well-written script could have done without any of the protocol's overhead. A stubborn person, in that stretch, keeps going because stopping would mean admitting the original bet was wrong, and admitting that costs more, to the ego, than continuing to sink evenings into a project that's stalled. Someone with actual conviction, as distinct from stubbornness, asks a harder, less comfortable question: is the thing I was originally right about still true, independent of how the project happens to be going this particular month?

He asks it. The honest answer, arrived at without much drama, is yes — the underlying gap MCP addresses is still real, still unfilled by anything better, and the stall in his own project says more about his available evening hours that particular month than it says about whether the bet was sound. He keeps going. Not out of stubbornness dressed as persistence. Because the reasoning that got him here in the first place still holds up when he checks it again, honestly, from a position of being willing to have found otherwise.

## 47. M.Tech at Eleven

There's a question he gets asked, occasionally, by people who find out he's doing a master's degree in AI while already seven years into a career and already, by most measures, doing work that would qualify as AI-adjacent without any additional credential attached to it: why bother.

It's a fair question, and the honest answer is less noble than the one that would sound better at a dinner party. It isn't primarily about the credential — a résumé line, a slightly stronger negotiating position, the kind of return-on-investment case a career coach would ask him to make if he pitched the decision that way. Some of that is real, and he won't pretend it isn't a factor. But it isn't the reason he's still awake at eleven p.m. on a Tuesday, three years into a job that already uses most of what the program teaches, working through a problem set on policy gradients he could, if he were being purely strategic about his time, reasonably skip.

The actual reason is closer to something he's slightly embarrassed to say plainly: seven years of practical experience gives you a strong, well-earned intuition for what works, and almost no exposure, most of the time, to why it works — the actual mathematics underneath the libraries, the theory that a working data pipeline lets you skip entirely because PySpark abstracts it away, the derivations a bootcamp would never require and a job, once you're competent enough to be trusted with real systems, stops demanding of you either. He got good at using tools without ever being forced to understand their insides, and at some point that gap started to bother him more than it should have, given how little it was actually costing him professionally.

The M.Tech, in that light, isn't really about becoming more employable. It's about closing a particular, private discomfort — the sense of being fluent in a language without knowing its grammar, competent without being able to explain, from first principles, why the thing he does every day actually works the way it does. Nobody at work has ever asked him to derive the Bellman equation. He's not sure anyone at work would care if he could. He cares, which is a less impressive-sounding reason than most people expect when they ask, and the truest one he has.

There's a cost to this that he doesn't dress up as noble either. The M.Tech makes him, most Tuesdays, exhausted in a specific compounding way — professionally competent all day, then academically a beginner all evening, holding both states in the same stretch of hours without much of a seam between them. He has, more than once, sat through a lecture on a concept his job already uses correctly in practice and felt the particular, disorienting vertigo of being simultaneously expert and novice about the exact same subject, depending on which hour of the day you asked him.

He's stopped trying to resolve that vertigo into something tidier. Some nights it's just true: he's both. The badge-off version of him and the badge-on version aren't actually different people pursuing different goals. They're the same person, asking the same question — is this actually correct, and how would I prove it — of two different subjects that happen, more and more, to be converging into the same one.

## 48. Being Wrong About the Model

He was wrong, for a while, about something specific enough to be worth admitting plainly rather than gesturing at vaguely: he assumed, early in the RAG pipeline's development, that a more sophisticated retrieval approach would obviously beat a simpler one, and built accordingly, spending real hours on a hybrid retrieval scheme — dense embeddings combined with a sparse keyword component, re-ranked by a secondary model — on the theory that more machinery meant more accuracy, an assumption that felt so self-evidently true he never actually tested the alternative before building past it.

The alternative, when he finally got around to testing it properly, out of nothing more than the same reconciliation habit that catches most of his mistakes — measure the thing before assuming you know the answer — was a plain, unglamorous keyword search over the same documents, no embeddings, no re-ranking, none of the machinery he'd spent the better part of a week assembling. On his actual evaluation set, for his actual documents, it performed within a rounding error of the sophisticated version. Not on every category of question — the hybrid approach genuinely won on a particular subset involving paraphrased queries where the exact keywords didn't appear in the source text. But on the majority of real questions his evaluation set contained, the machinery he'd been proud of was buying almost nothing.

This stung more than a purely technical mistake should sting, and he's tried, since, to understand why. Part of it is straightforward sunk cost, the same force that keeps anyone attached to an approach they've already invested real hours in, resistant to evidence that the investment wasn't as necessary as it felt while making it. But part of it is something closer to identity, uncomfortable to name directly: he'd built the sophisticated version because building the sophisticated version felt like the kind of thing a person who takes this seriously does, and discovering that the simple version worked almost as well felt, for a beat, like a small accusation — that maybe the sophistication had been more about proving something to himself than about actually solving the problem in front of him.

He kept the hybrid approach, in the end, because the subset of questions it genuinely won on was worth winning, and because by the time he'd properly measured the tradeoff, he had the actual evidence to justify the added complexity instead of just the assumption. That's the part that matters, more than which approach he ultimately shipped. He didn't stay wrong. He built something on an assumption, tested the assumption properly once he had reason to doubt it, found it partially false, and adjusted — not defensively, not by finding a way to declare the test invalid, just by updating, plainly, and moving forward with a version of the decision that could now survive being questioned.

That update is the part he's actually proud of, if pride is the right word for something this small. Not that he was right the first time — he wasn't. That being wrong, once demonstrated clearly enough, changed what he did next.

## 49. Conviction Is Not Certainty

If there's a single thread running under this whole part of the book — the QA years, the coordinator role nobody put much weight on, the bet on a young protocol, the degree he didn't strictly need, the retrieval approach he had to walk halfway back from — it's not really about career strategy, and he'd resist anyone reading it that way. It's about the specific, unglamorous discipline of holding a belief firmly enough to act on it and loosely enough to let it go the moment the evidence actually turns against it.

Those two things sound like they should be in tension, and most of the time they are. The whole architecture of confidence, professionally, socially, culturally, rewards the appearance of certainty — a person who states an opinion firmly and never revises it reads, to most rooms, as more competent than a person who says "here's what I currently think, and here's what would change my mind," even though the second person is very often the one actually tracking reality more closely. He has caught himself, more than once, performing the first kind of confidence in a meeting because the second kind takes longer to explain and nobody in the room has the patience for the longer version.

Late at night, alone, with nobody in the room to perform for, that pressure mostly disappears, and what's left is closer to what conviction actually is when it isn't being watched: not a fixed position, a currently-best position, held with enough seriousness to build on top of and enough humility to abandon the moment it stops holding up. The MCP bet survives that test — checked honestly, it still holds. The sophisticated retrieval approach didn't survive it fully intact — checked honestly, it needed adjusting, and got adjusted.

He doesn't think this makes him unusually wise. If anything, it's closer to the opposite — a coping mechanism for someone who's been wrong often enough, in small enough ways, across enough different domains, that pretending otherwise stopped being worth the energy it takes to maintain. The QA instinct, again, wearing a new coat: not just is this data correct, but is this belief of mine still correct, checked the same way, on the same schedule, with the same willingness to find out it isn't.

Nobody hands out a certificate for this. There's no line on a résumé for "updated his position on retrieval architecture after eleven hours of unglamorous testing." It doesn't compress into a story with a clean moral, and he's suspicious, by now, of any account of his own thinking that does compress that cleanly — suspicious enough that he's stopped trying to force one. What's true, plainly, without a lesson wrapped around it, is smaller than a moral: some of his bets have held up. Some of them haven't. He's still finding out, in real time, which category most of the current ones belong to, and he has made a kind of peace with not knowing yet.
