# PART III — 3:00 AM

## 11. An Empty Folder

`mkdir tki-agent` is not a significant sentence. It is eleven characters, most of them the project name, and it produces, on his screen, a folder with nothing in it — no files, no history, no evidence that anything is about to happen here at all. And yet there is a particular optimism attached to the moment right before `cd` into that folder, an optimism he has felt enough times now to recognize as chemical rather than rational. Every project is perfect in the second before it contains any code.

The name doesn't mean much, if he's honest about it. TKI. He picked it fast, on the theory that a bad name attached to a project that gets built beats a perfect name attached to a project that doesn't, and he has watched enough side projects die in the naming phase — a Notion page full of candidate names, none committed to, the actual idea slowly cooling under the weight of finding the right word for it — to trust that theory completely. TKI can mean something later. Or never. Plenty of his repositories have names that meant something to him for exactly one evening in whatever year he created them, and now sit in his GitHub profile as small unexplained artifacts, initials nobody will ever ask him to decode.

What matters more than the name is the shape of the thing he's trying to build, and the shape, at ten-forty on a Wednesday, is still mostly vibes. An agent. Something that can hold a goal and reach for tools to accomplish it, using MCP instead of the usual bespoke integration work — instead of writing, by hand, yet another wrapper function that calls yet another API in yet another slightly different way than the last three times he's done this. He wants to build one thing and have it be able to reach a growing list of tools without him touching the core logic every time the list grows. That's the whole pitch, the one-sentence version he'd give if someone asked, which nobody is going to, because it's ten-forty on a Wednesday and the only audience for this sentence is him.

He opens a terminal. `python -m venv .venv`. The unglamorous ritual before any Python project gets to be interesting — an environment, isolated, empty, waiting to be filled with packages that will mostly work and occasionally conflict with each other in ways that cost forty minutes to untangle later. He doesn't know yet that tonight isn't going to be one of those nights. Nobody knows that in advance. That's rather the point of starting.

`pip install mcp`. It installs cleanly, which feels like a small piece of good luck, though it's really just a sign that the ecosystem around this particular protocol is young enough and small enough that dependency conflicts haven't had time to accumulate yet. Give it two years. Give it the fate of every popular Python package, which is to eventually require you to pin four other packages to specific patch versions just to get the thing to import.

For now, though: clean install, empty folder, a name that doesn't mean anything yet, and the particular quiet of a house where everyone else's part of the evening has already happened. This is the part of the process nobody puts in a portfolio, because there's nothing to screenshot. An empty folder looks the same as every other empty folder. The value of the moment is entirely internal, entirely about the fact that ten minutes ago this project didn't exist and now, technically, it does — a directory, a virtual environment, a single dependency, and an idea with nowhere left to hide from being tested.

He writes the first file. `main.py`. Two lines: an import, and a `print("hello")`. It runs. It prints "hello." This is, if you are being extremely honest about it, not an accomplishment. It is the least impressive thing a computer can be asked to do. And there is still, every single time, a small flicker of satisfaction in watching it happen — proof that the environment works, that the interpreter is where it's supposed to be, that nothing about tonight's setup is broken before the actual problem has even shown up to be solved.

He has built enough things now to know that this feeling — the hello-world feeling — doesn't scale. It's not going to sustain four hours of work on its own. But it's a fine place to start from, and starting from a fine place, he has learned the hard way, matters more than people give it credit for. Most of what kills a side project isn't difficulty. It's friction in the first twenty minutes, compounding into a reason to check Twitter instead.

Tonight, twenty minutes in, there's no friction yet. Just an empty folder that isn't empty anymore, and a cursor blinking on line three of a file that's about to get considerably longer.

## 12. Hello, Tool

The MCP specification, read at nine p.m. as an abstract diagram, turns out to be a completely different object at eleven p.m. when you're trying to make the diagram actually run. This is true of every specification he has ever worked from, in every field adjacent to software, and it never stops being slightly humbling: the gap between understanding an idea and implementing it is not a gap you can read your way across. You have to walk it, badly, one broken attempt at a time.

The first tool he wants to build is deliberately trivial — a function that returns the current time. Not because an agent that can tell the time is useful. Because it's the smallest possible unit of "the model asks for something real and gets something real back," and if that unit doesn't work, nothing built on top of it is going to work either. Test the foundation before you build the house. It's the same instinct that used to make him write the simplest possible test case first, back when the job title had the word Quality in it — establish the floor before you worry about the ceiling.

He writes the tool. Registers it with the server. Points a small test client at it. And gets, on the first attempt, a beautifully unhelpful error: a stack trace three levels deep, ending in a complaint about a schema mismatch that gives no indication of which field, on which side, disagrees with which other field.

This is the part of building that never makes it into anyone's highlight reel, and it is also, if he's honest, most of what building actually is. Not the moment the thing works. The forty minutes before that, spent adding print statements at every layer of a system that's supposed to be talking to itself and clearly isn't, narrowing the search space one `print(f"HERE: {x}")` at a time until the actual disagreement reveals itself: the tool's return type doesn't match what the schema declared it would be, a mismatch so small — a dictionary where a specific object type was expected — that the error message could have said so directly and chose, for reasons no error message has ever adequately explained, not to.

He fixes it. One line. The kind of fix that makes the forty minutes preceding it feel, in retrospect, faintly ridiculous — as if the computer had been sitting there the whole time, perfectly capable of saying "your return type is wrong on line 14," and had simply decided not to, out of some inscrutable error-message-design philosophy that prioritizes technical accuracy over being even slightly useful to the human reading it at eleven-forty at night.

He runs the client again. The tool call goes out. The tool executes. The current time comes back, correctly, formatted exactly the way he asked it to be, and lands in the model's response as if it had always known what time it was — which it hadn't, five minutes ago, and couldn't have, on its own, no matter how large or well-trained it was. This is the whole magic trick, laid bare: not that the model got smarter, but that it stopped needing to be, because now there's a tool for the parts it was never going to be good at anyway.

It's not much. A working clock, wired into a chat interface, at a level of complexity a competent developer could reproduce from scratch in twenty minutes flat, not two and a half hours. But it is, structurally, the whole pattern — the same pattern that will, several tools later, let this agent reach further than "what time is it" into places that actually matter to him.

He sits back. It's a few minutes before midnight. Somewhere in his contract with his employer, none of this counts for anything — no ticket references it, no standup will mention it, the twenty-percent efficiency number on his résumé has nothing to do with a clock tool that took two and a half hours to make work correctly. And he is, at this exact moment, more satisfied than he was at any point during the actual workday.

He doesn't examine that feeling too closely. He's learned, over enough nights like this one, that examining it too closely tends to produce an answer that sounds better on a slide than it feels in the room — something about ownership, about building versus maintaining, about audience versus its absence. All true, probably. None of it is what he's thinking at midnight. What he's thinking at midnight is: what's the next tool.

## 13. The Minimal ChatGPT Clone

Before the agent, before MCP entered the picture at all, there was a smaller, dumber project — one he still has sitting in a repository titled, without irony, "minimal-chatgpt-clone," because at the time that's exactly what it was trying to be. Not a product. Not even a very good clone. Just an attempt to answer a question that had been bothering him for weeks: underneath the polished interface everyone uses every day, what is actually happening, mechanically, when you send a message and watch the words appear one at a time?

The honest answer, it turns out, is almost insultingly ordinary. You send a prompt to an API. You get back either a complete block of text or, if you ask nicely with the right parameter, a stream of small chunks — tokens, or pieces of tokens, arriving one after another over the same kind of connection that used to carry stock tickers and now carries this instead. The illusion of a mind composing a thought in real time is, underneath, a for-loop printing chunks to a terminal slightly slower than instantaneous. He knew this abstractly before he built it. Knowing it abstractly and watching your own for-loop produce the exact same illusion are different kinds of knowing.

The streaming part came together fast, faster than he expected, which should have been a warning. It's the second requirement — multi-turn memory, the ability for the model to remember what you said three messages ago — that turned out to be the actual project, hiding behind a feature that sounds, from the outside, like it should be trivial.

Here is the part that made him laugh, once he understood it properly: there is no memory. Not in any sense a human would recognize the word. The model doesn't remember the previous message any more than a photocopier remembers the page it copied yesterday. What actually happens is considerably less dignified: every single time you send a new message, his code quietly gathers up the entire conversation so far — every message either of you has sent — glues it back together into one long document, and sends the whole thing again, from the beginning, as if for the first time. The model isn't remembering the conversation. It's re-reading the entire transcript on every single turn, cover to cover, and responding as though it had always known it.

It works. That's the strange part. It works so well that the illusion of a continuous mind holding a conversation is completely convincing, right up until you build it yourself and see the trick from backstage — a string, growing longer every turn, being handed wholesale to a system with no persistent memory of anything, that generates the appearance of continuity purely by being shown its own past every single time it's asked to speak again.

There's a joke in there somewhere about consciousness, or about relationships, or about how most conversations — human ones included — run on exactly this mechanism, a story you keep re-telling yourself about what's already been said, rather than any genuine unbroken thread of experience. He doesn't chase the joke very far. It's past midnight, and the actual engineering problem in front of him is more immediate and much less philosophical: the transcript keeps growing, and eventually it's going to get too long to send.

## 14. The Bill Arrives Eventually

Every API that streams tokens back to you is also, quietly, counting them, and every token — his side and the model's — costs a small fraction of a cent that means nothing on message four and starts meaning something on message forty, once a long conversation has accumulated enough history that every new message drags the entire ballooning transcript along with it like a coat that keeps getting heavier every time you put it back on.

This is the unglamorous middle of the multi-turn memory problem, the part that never makes it into a demo video: at some point, the conversation has to forget something, on purpose, in a way that doesn't make the model seem like it's lost the plot. He tries the obvious approach first — just chop off the oldest messages once the transcript passes some length, first in, first out, like a queue. It works, technically. It also produces a particular and slightly comic failure mode: ask the model to recall something from early in a long conversation, and it will confidently, fluently, tell you something that isn't true, because the actual information scrolled off the back of the buffer twenty messages ago and nobody told the model it was supposed to say "I don't remember that" instead of just generating something plausible-sounding to fill the gap.

This is the first time he runs headlong into a problem that is going to define an entire year of his side projects and a fair portion of his coursework: a language model does not know what it doesn't know. It doesn't experience the absence of information as a gap. It experiences it as an invitation to generate something equally fluent in its place. Ask a human what they had for lunch three weeks ago and most will say, correctly, "I have no idea." Ask a model the equivalent question, with the actual answer trimmed out of its context window, and it will often just tell you — wrong, cheerful, indistinguishable in tone from a correct answer.

He doesn't solve this properly that night. Nobody solves it properly in one night; entire companies exist to chip away at exactly this problem, with teams and budgets he doesn't have. What he does is smaller and, in its own way, more honest: he adds a running summary, a compressed paragraph that gets updated every few turns to hold onto the gist of what came before, so that even after the raw messages fall off the back of the buffer, something survives. It's a patch, not a fix. It loses detail. It's the conversational equivalent of Silver instead of Gold — cleaner than the raw mess, still missing things a full reconciliation would catch.

He knows the analogy is a stretch even as he thinks it, and thinks it anyway, because by one in the morning most of his private vocabulary for new problems is borrowed from the vocabulary of his day job, whether it fits perfectly or not. Bronze, silver, gold. Raw, cleaned, trustworthy. He is apparently unable to stop being a data engineer even in a project that has nothing to do with his employer, at an hour his employer has no claim on. That might be the closest thing to an identity all of this is going to locate: not a job, not a title, just a way of asking the same question about whatever's currently in front of him.

## 15. Teaching a Model to Cite Its Sources

The RAG pipeline starts, like most of his projects seem to, as a reaction to something that annoyed him. Specifically: the ease with which a fluent model will answer a question about a document it has never actually read, using nothing but pattern-matched confidence, in a tone completely indistinguishable from a model that read the document carefully and is now accurately summarizing it. There's no tell. No hedge in the voice. Just two answers, one grounded and one invented, delivered with identical certainty.

Retrieval-augmented generation is the industry's answer to this, and the idea, again, is simpler than the acronym makes it sound. Instead of trusting the model to know things, you give it a library card. You take a pile of documents, chop them into chunks small enough to be useful and large enough to still make sense out of context, convert each chunk into a long list of numbers that captures something about its meaning — an embedding, in the jargon, though "meaning as coordinates" is closer to what's actually happening — and store all of that in a structure built for exactly one operation: given a question, find the chunks whose coordinates are nearest to it. Then you hand the model those chunks, and only those chunks, and ask it to answer using what's actually there, not what it remembers from training.

He builds this with LangChain doing the orchestration, Hugging Face's embedding models turning text into coordinates, and Llama running the actual generation once the relevant chunks have been retrieved. None of these choices are especially original — this exact stack, in this exact combination, has been built by thousands of people this year alone, a fact he's aware of and finds oddly comforting rather than discouraging. He's not trying to invent RAG. He's trying to understand it by making his own broken version of it, which turns out to be a completely different kind of understanding than reading about someone else's working version.

The chunking is where it first goes wrong, in a way that's almost funny once he catches it. He splits documents by a fixed character count, which seems reasonable until he notices that a sentence explaining a critical exception to a rule has been sliced neatly in half — the rule in one chunk, the exception dangling alone in the next, its antecedent gone. Retrieved on its own, the exception chunk reads like an unexplained contradiction. The model, doing exactly what it's supposed to do with the information it's given, produces an answer that's technically grounded in the retrieved text and completely wrong about what the document actually says, because the document, sliced that way, doesn't say anything coherent anymore. He hadn't broken the model. He'd broken the evidence before it ever reached the model, and then blamed the witness.

He adjusts the chunking to respect sentence boundaries, adds a small overlap between adjacent chunks so an idea that spans a boundary doesn't vanish entirely from either side, and the failure rate drops — not to zero, nothing drops to zero in this field, but enough that he stops seeing the exact same mistake repeat itself on every third query. This is where the QA instinct earns its keep again, the same instinct that used to live inside a Jira ticket: he doesn't just eyeball a handful of answers and declare victory. He builds a small, ugly, unglamorous evaluation set — a list of questions with known correct answers, run automatically after every change, checked not by feeling but by comparison. It is, more or less, a test suite. He didn't call it that on purpose. He called it that because it's exactly what it is, wearing a newer name.

Somewhere in a lecture recording he half-watched two weeks earlier, a professor mentioned RAGAS — a framework built specifically to score retrieval-augmented systems on things like faithfulness, whether the answer actually follows from the retrieved text, and relevance, whether the retrieved text was even the right text to begin with. He hadn't thought much of it at the time, filed under "will matter eventually." It matters now, at half past one in the morning, because it gives a vague, deeply satisfying feeling — *this seems better than yesterday* — an actual number to attach itself to. Faithfulness up. Context relevance up. Two metrics, moving in the direction he wanted, for reasons he can mostly explain.

He doesn't build anything a company would pay for tonight. What he builds is a small, private answer to a question that had been bothering him since before he had the vocabulary to ask it precisely: not just *can a model answer questions about a document,* but *how do you prove it's actually reading, instead of guessing well.* Same question, again, in new clothes. It followed him from a QA desk in an earlier job all the way to a laptop at 1:40 a.m., and it doesn't look like it's planning to leave anytime soon.
