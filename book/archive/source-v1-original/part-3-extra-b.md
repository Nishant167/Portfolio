## Tokens, Not Words

The NLP module, several weeks into the M.Tech, does something to his relationship with language that he doesn't fully anticipate going in: it makes ordinary sentences briefly, uncomfortably strange, the way learning the etymology of a word you've used your whole life can make the word itself feel unfamiliar for a day or two afterward.

A model doesn't read words. This is the first thing the module insists on, patiently, in a way that suggests the professor has explained it to enough confused students that the patience is well-practiced. It reads tokens — fragments, not always aligned with anything a human would recognize as a meaningful unit, sometimes a whole common word, sometimes half of a rarer one, sometimes a single character standing alone because the tokenizer never learned the larger chunk it belongs to. "Reconciliation," a word he types more often professionally than almost anyone he knows, doesn't necessarily arrive at the model as one token. It might arrive as two, or three, stitched together from pieces the tokenizer learned from a training corpus that had its own opinions about which fragments of English show up often enough to deserve their own dedicated slot.

He builds a small script, mostly out of curiosity rather than any assignment requiring it, that takes a sentence and shows him exactly how it gets split — a toy, really, the kind of thing that produces no lasting artifact and exists purely to satisfy a question he couldn't stop having. He runs his own name through it. Runs the name of his employer, the one he's not allowed to write here either, through it. Runs a line from a git commit message — `Fix: Set basePath to /Portfolio for GitHub Pages asset loading` — through it, and watches the sentence break into a sequence of fragments that bear a strange, slightly comic relationship to the words he actually typed, `base` and `Path` as two separate pieces, `Git` `Hub` split cleanly at the internal capital the way he never would have predicted a purely statistical process to notice, `.md` treated as its own small, self-contained unit, as if the tokenizer had quietly learned that markdown files are a particular enough category of thing to deserve a shortcut.

It's a small discovery with a large downstream consequence, once he actually absorbs it: every strange thing he's ever seen a language model do with numbers, with unusual formatting, with code that mixes conventions — snake_case next to camelCase, an acronym next to a word it happens to share letters with — traces back, at least partly, to this same layer, the tokenizer deciding, long before any of the actual "understanding" happens, how to chop the input into pieces the rest of the model was trained to work with. A model that seems to struggle with basic arithmetic isn't necessarily bad at math in the way a person would be bad at math. It's frequently just seeing the numbers in pieces that don't line up cleanly with place value, "1247" arriving as some awkward combination of fragments that never quite reconstitutes into the clean mental number a human reads at a glance.

This changes, in a small but real way, how he debugs the RAG pipeline afterward. A retrieval failure he'd previously chalked up to a vague "the embedding didn't capture the meaning well enough" starts to look, some of the time, like something more specific and more fixable: a technical term getting split in a way that scattered its meaning across fragments the embedding model never learned to recombine cleanly. It's not a universal fix. Most of the pipeline's failures aren't tokenization artifacts; most of them are exactly the chunking and retrieval issues he'd already been chasing for weeks. But a few of them are, and finding even a few gives him a new category of question to ask when something breaks, which turns out, more than any single fix, to be the actual lasting value of the module — not a technique, a new place to look.

## Orchestrating a Container That Doesn't Want to Talk to Itself

Kubernetes enters the picture not because the ChatGPT clone, a toy project with an audience of one friend on one evening, has any legitimate need for container orchestration, but because it's on the skill list at work, listed dryly next to Docker and AKS as though the two were a natural pair rather than an entirely different order of complexity, and he's decided, with the specific stubbornness that governs most of his side-project choices, that he'd rather learn it badly on something low-stakes than learn it for the first time on something a client's data depends on.

The mental model, once he finally gets past the wall of new vocabulary — pods, services, deployments, ingress, a whole taxonomy of nouns that all sound like they should be simple and combine into something that very much isn't at first — turns out to be almost elegant, once it clicks. A pod is the smallest deployable unit, one or more containers that always travel together. A deployment describes how many copies of a pod should exist and how to replace them when they fail or need updating. A service gives a stable way to reach a set of pods even as the individual pods underneath it get destroyed and recreated, an abstraction that exists specifically because pods are supposed to be disposable, treated as livestock rather than pets, a phrase from the documentation that makes him laugh the first time he reads it and that he now can't stop thinking in whenever a server crashes and restarts cleanly without anyone needing to intervene.

Getting a local cluster running on his own machine, using a lightweight tool built for exactly this kind of learning rather than production use, is straightforward enough. Getting his ChatGPT clone's container to actually run happily inside it takes considerably longer, mostly because the failure modes at this layer are unfamiliar enough that his usual debugging instincts — the ones honed over years of application-level bugs — don't map cleanly onto them. A pod that won't start doesn't throw a stack trace he recognizes. It sits in a state called `CrashLoopBackOff`, a name that sounds, read cold, less like a technical status and more like the description of an actual emotional experience, and figuring out why requires reading logs from a layer he's never had to read logs from before, digging past his own application's output into the orchestration system's own account of what it tried to do and why it gave up.

The eventual cause, once found, is almost funny in its smallness: a health check configured to hit an endpoint his application didn't actually expose, so Kubernetes, dutifully following instructions he'd given it without fully understanding what they meant, kept declaring the pod unhealthy and restarting it, over and over, a container stuck in a loop of being killed for failing a test it was never actually built to pass. He adds the missing endpoint — three lines of code, a route that returns "ok" and nothing else — and the crash loop stops immediately, the pod settling into the calm, boring, successful state that's supposed to be the entire point of all this infrastructure in the first place.

He doesn't keep the cluster running past that evening; there's no reason to, for a toy project with one user, and the local Kubernetes setup gets torn down as casually as it was built. What survives is the vocabulary, and more importantly, the particular, hard-won intuition for what an orchestration layer is actually protecting against — not cleverness, mostly, just the ordinary, unglamorous inevitability that something, eventually, is going to crash, and the entire discipline exists to make sure that when it does, nobody has to be awake, watching, ready to restart it by hand.
