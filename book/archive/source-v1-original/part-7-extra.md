## The Pipeline That Deploys the Pipelines

There's a specific irony in his job title that took him a while to notice properly, though once noticed it's hard to stop seeing: he builds data pipelines for a living, and the process by which those pipelines themselves get built, tested, and deployed is also, structurally, a pipeline — Azure DevOps orchestrating a sequence of stages that take his PySpark code from a feature branch to production, checking, transforming, and validating at each step, in a shape that would look, on a whiteboard, almost identical to the medallion architecture the code itself implements once it's actually running.

He didn't design this system; he inherited most of it, the way most engineers inherit most of their CI/CD setups, arriving into a company's existing conventions rather than architecting them from a blank slate. What he's done, over the years, is extend it — adding stages, tightening gates, occasionally ripping out a step that had outlived whatever problem it was originally built to solve, the same slow accretion and pruning that governs every long-lived piece of infrastructure he's ever worked with, professional or personal.

The gate he's proudest of, if pride is the right word for something this quietly unglamorous, is a validation stage he added roughly a year into the role: an automated reconciliation check that runs against every pull request touching the pipeline code, comparing a small sample of output against a known-good baseline before the change is allowed to merge. It exists because of an incident that predates his involvement in this particular gate, one he only knows secondhand, from a teammate's account rather than his own memory — a change that looked correct, passed the existing tests, which only checked that the code ran without errors rather than that its output was actually right, and shipped a subtly wrong transformation into production for the better part of a week before someone downstream noticed a number that didn't look right.

"The code ran without errors" and "the code produced correct results" are, he has learned across years of exactly this kind of near-miss, two claims that feel like they should be the same thing and very much are not. A test suite that only checks the first is checking the easy half of the problem — the half a compiler or an interpreter would have caught anyway, sooner or later, on its own. The genuinely dangerous failures, the ones that cost a client's trust rather than just an afternoon of debugging, are the ones that run perfectly, produce a plausible-looking result, and are simply, quietly wrong in a way that no amount of clean execution would ever reveal.

Building the automated reconciliation gate meant, in practice, formalizing exactly the instinct these pages keeps circling back to — is this actually correct, made structural, made mandatory, made impossible to skip under deadline pressure the way a manual check, performed by an exhausted human at the end of a long week, is always at risk of being skipped or rushed. The gate doesn't care how tired anyone is. It runs the same comparison, at the same rigor, every single time, on every single change, regardless of how confident the author felt writing it.

He thinks of this gate, some nights, as the professional twin of the evening's much smaller, much less consequential RAG evaluation dashboard — two instruments built for the exact same underlying reason, at wildly different scales and stakes, both existing because he's learned, the hard way, more than once, that a system's confidence in its own correctness and its actual correctness are two separate claims that need to be checked separately, every time, by something more disciplined than a hopeful glance at a green checkmark.

## Ninety-Nine Point Nine

The uptime figure on the portfolio — ninety-nine point nine percent, rendered as a small stat on the hero section, sitting next to the medallion label as if the two were equally decorative — is, unlike the invented skill-mix percentages and the freely chosen progress ring, an actual number, measured, real, and true of the systems he maintains, which he included specifically because it was one of the only figures on the entire site he could put there without any of the self-conscious hedging that governs almost every other number on the page.

Ninety-nine point nine percent sounds, to someone who hasn't spent years thinking about availability numbers, close enough to perfect that the remaining zero point one might as well not exist. It does exist, and it matters more than the rounding suggests: over a year, ninety-nine point nine percent uptime allows for roughly nine hours of downtime total, distributed however the failures happen to land, and nine hours, concentrated in the wrong nine-hour window, can be the difference between a maintenance blip nobody notices and an outage that costs a client real, attributable money during a campaign's most critical reporting period.

Getting to that number, and more importantly staying there, involves considerably less heroism than the figure implies sitting alone on a hero section. It's mostly the boring disciplines he has spent a lot of time describing under other names: reconciliation checks that catch problems before they reach anyone who'd notice, a CI/CD gate that refuses to ship code whose output doesn't match a known-good baseline, monitoring that pages someone — occasionally him, at hours no less inconvenient than the ones he has spent describing for entirely voluntary projects — the moment a pipeline's run time drifts far enough from its historical pattern to suggest something's wrong before a client's dashboard ever shows a gap.

There's an occasional, quiet strangeness in noticing how much overlap exists between the discipline that produces a boring, reliable ninety-nine point nine percent at work and the discipline that produces a working TKI agent tool call at midnight, on a project nobody's paying for and no uptime figure will ever be calculated for. Both come from the same insistence on verifying rather than assuming, on building the check before trusting the result, on treating "it seems to work" as a hypothesis rather than a conclusion. He didn't set out, seven years ago, to develop a single coherent professional philosophy that would apply equally to enterprise advertising infrastructure and to a hobby project exploring a protocol that didn't exist until recently. It happened anyway, the way most of the actual continuity in a working life happens — not planned, just accumulated, one reconciled number at a time, until the accumulation started looking, from far enough away, like something almost as deliberate as a plan would have been.
